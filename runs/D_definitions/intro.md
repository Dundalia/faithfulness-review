# Definitions of Faithfulness, Counterfactual and Monitorability

This site maps research on chain-of-thought (CoT) faithfulness and monitorability, and "the map" means its collection of papers. This page collects how the papers in the map define three terms: *faithfulness*, *counterfactual* and *monitorability*. It is based on 3,414 definitions quoted from 216 of the 217 papers that the map held when this collection of definitions was built. The papers on formal frameworks that were added to the map on 1 November 2026 have their own collection of definitions, in the Formal tab of the dashboard and on the [formal frameworks page](page.html?doc=formal).

Each definition is quoted word for word, with its location in the paper, and is labeled with a kind and a stance. The kind says what sort of definition it is:

- *informal*: stated in words;
- *formal*: stated mathematically;
- *operational*: a test, metric or procedure that measures or labels the notion;
- *criterion*: a necessary or sufficient condition for the notion, or an assumption it rests on;
- *failure mode*: a named way in which the notion fails, such as a type of unfaithful reasoning;
- *taxonomy*: a division into types.

The stance says where the definition comes from. Either the paper *proposes* it, or the paper borrows it from earlier work and *adopts* it unchanged or *adapts* it. For a borrowed definition, the collection records the source: 1,224 of the 1,225 borrowed definitions name one. The full set is in [`definitions.csv`](https://github.com/Dundalia/faithfulness-review/blob/main/definitions.csv), a table with one row per definition, and the Definitions tab of the dashboard lets you filter it.

For each term, the definitions are sorted into groups by meaning: a group collects definitions with a similar meaning. Each term's section then traces which papers borrow which definition from which earlier papers, checks the borrowed definitions against their sources, and lists the points on which papers disagree. In the tables, text in quotation marks is copied from the paper. Text without quotation marks is a short paraphrase, not a quote.

The table below gives, for each term, the number of definitions, the number of papers they come from, the number of groups, and the main way the definitions divide. Here and below, the trace is the model's written reasoning (its CoT).

| Term | Definitions | Papers | Groups | Main division |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Faithfulness](#faithfulness) | 1,955 | 204 | 11 | Nine relations: disclosure, causal dependence, agreement, say-do consistency, prediction, self-report accuracy, input importance, internal computation, honesty |
| [Counterfactual](#counterfactual) | 292 | 87 | 12 | What is varied, whether the output must change, how a change becomes a causal claim, and what the comparison judges |
| [Monitorability](#monitorability) | 1,167 | 108 | 18 | A monitor's ability to predict a property, or the trace's verbalization of what drove the answer |

## Main findings

**Faithfulness has one common definition and nine relations that papers test, and the relations can disagree.** [Jacovi & Goldberg 2020](./#p=jacovi2020towards) define a faithful interpretation as one that "accurately represents the reasoning process behind the model’s prediction", and 79 definitions from 55 papers cite them. Groups 2 to 10 of the faithfulness section distinguish nine relations. They ask what the trace discloses, whether the answer depends on the trace or agrees with it, whether an action matches what was said, and whether the explanation lets an observer predict the model. They also ask whether a self-report matches the model's behavior or internal states, whether the inputs that the explanation marks as important matter to the prediction, whether the trace matches the internal computation, and whether a statement matches the model's belief. Some of these relations relax the common definition. [Baker et al. 2025](./#p=baker2025monitoring) consider one property $v$ and require that "$z$ is a sufficient statistic for $v$", which means that $z$ (for example a CoT) contains all the information relevant to predicting $v$. [Viteri et al. 2024](./#p=viteri2026markovian) prioritize "informativeness over full faithfulness". The relations can disagree on the same traces, and so can different classifiers of a single relation. In a hint test, a hint is inserted into the prompt, and a classifier decides whether the trace acknowledges it. On identical data, the three hint-acknowledgment classifiers of [Young 2026a](./#p=young2026measuring) give overall faithfulness rates of 74.4%, 82.6% and 69.7%.

**Counterfactual definitions differ on four points.** They differ in what is changed to produce the variant (the counterfactual case), in whether the variant must change the model's output, in how a change in output is turned into a claim about cause, and in what the comparison of the original and the variant is used to judge: an explanation, the model's reliance on a feature, a self-report, a training target or a covert behavior. The section on counterfactuals lists ten points of disagreement that follow from these four differences. Among them are whether the variant must change the output, and whether one flipped answer (an answer that changes after an edit) shows that the edit caused it.

**Monitorability has two competing definitions, and they disagree on whether, and how far, monitorability requires faithfulness.** In 42 definitions from 24 papers, monitorability is a monitor's ability to predict a property of the model's behavior. In 69 definitions from 34 papers, it depends on whether the trace verbalizes (states in words) what drove the answer. 9 papers have definitions in both groups. [Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring) write that their notion of monitorability "does not require a model’s externalized reasoning to faithfully reflect its internal reasoning process", and [Baker et al. 2025](./#p=baker2025monitoring) require faithfulness only with respect to the monitored property. [Jia et al. 2026](./#p=jia2026faithfulness), [Xiong et al. 2026](./#p=xiong2026monitorability) and [Anonymous 2026 (one draw)](./#p=anon2026one) state the faithfulness requirement without that restriction.

**In 82% of the checks, the cited source contains the borrowed definition.** Borrowed definitions were checked against the sources they cite, when those sources are in the map. Of the 804 borrowed definitions that cite a source in the map, 803 were checked. This gives 1,095 checks, one for each pair of a definition and a source in the map that it cites. The one unchecked definition is the rubric that [Nguyen et al. 2026b](./#p=nguyen2026generalization) give a judge model for cue acknowledgment (whether the model's reasoning or response mentions a cue inserted into the prompt); it cites [Meek et al. 2025](./#p=meek2025measuring). Each check ends in a verdict. In 898 checks, the source gives the definition, either as the paper states it (verdict "supported", 647 checks) or in a form that the citing paper modifies, for example by narrowing or extending it (verdict "adapted", 251 checks). The other 197 checks have one of five verdicts: "partial" (122), when the source supports only part of the definition; "renamed" (43), when the source defines the same thing under another name; "other-claim" (13), when the citation supports a claim other than the definition, such as a finding stated next to it; "unsupported" (15), when the source does not give the definition; and "misdirected" (4), when the definition comes from a different paper than the one cited. The checks cover 803 of the 804 definitions that could be checked, so these are rates for the whole collection, not estimates from a sample. Among the faithfulness definitions, ten checks find a source credited with a definition it does not give, and in three of them the source is [Jacovi & Goldberg 2020](./#p=jacovi2020towards). Among the counterfactual definitions, 6 of the 15 that cite [Lanham et al. 2023](./#p=lanham2023measuring) fail the check, which means that their verdict is neither "supported" nor "adapted".

## How this page was made

1. **First collection of definitions, from excerpts.** A script (`scripts/definitions.py excerpt`) kept each section of a paper's full text that mentions one of the terms, leaving out the reference list and most appendices. Claude agents then quoted the definitions in those sections: 1,197 definitions. The full reading in step 2 showed that this missed definitions in methods sections and appendices.
2. **Second collection, from the full texts.** Agents read every paper in full, appendices included, and added, corrected or dropped definitions. The scope was also widened. For faithfulness, it now covers honesty, say-do consistency (whether a model's actions match what it said) and self-report accuracy (whether a model's reports about itself are true). For monitorability, it now covers scheming, covert actions, sabotage, sandbagging (deliberate underperformance), deception, reward hacking and verbalized evaluation awareness (a model saying in its reasoning that it may be under evaluation). Pure introspection (a model's access to its own internal states, studied as a capability and not as the accuracy of its reports about itself) is out of scope. So is faithfulness to a source document (as in summarization), unless the paper explicitly contrasts it with the notions above. To apply these exclusions, another model, GPT-6.1 Sol (run through Codex), screened the 130 definitions that a keyword search flagged and dropped 20. A check of a sample of 12 added definitions found 3 errors (one definition out of scope, and two with the wrong stance or source), and they are fixed (`checks/sample_audit_*.json`). Every one of the 3,414 quotes appears word for word in the paper's text, apart from differences in whitespace and curly quotes.
3. **Checking borrowed definitions against their sources.** For 803 of the 804 borrowed definitions whose source is in the map, an agent read the source and gave a verdict (1,095 checks of a definition against a cited source). The verdicts are recorded in the `attribution` column of the CSV file. Sources outside the map were not checked. One of them is Atanasova et al. (2023), which is cited by 18 counterfactual definitions from 14 papers. Of these, 13 definitions (from 10 papers) are in the group on the counterfactual test of explanations, which asks whether an explanation mentions an edit to the input that changed the prediction.
4. **Grouping the definitions and writing the sections.** For each term, one agent placed every definition in exactly one group (the `cluster` column of the CSV file) and wrote the term's section. It also listed each factual claim that the section makes: 607 claims in all.
5. **Two independent checks of every claim.** GPT-6.1 Sol checked each claim against the papers' full texts and recomputed every count. 95 claims were wrong (38), overstated (52) or understated (5), and they were replaced by the strongest statement that the evidence supports. Claude Opus then checked every claim again, without seeing those verdicts, and corrected 54 more (19 wrong, 25 overstated, 10 understated). Of these 54, 43 had passed the first check. A last round of editing fixed references and counts that span more than one sentence. The 15 sentences it reworded were checked a third time, and 3 were corrected. On a sample of 31 claims, two independent runs of the first checker found 7 and 6 errors, 5 of them found by both runs (`checks/calibration_*.json`). So a single check misses some errors. The second check reduces this risk but does not remove it. The prompts of every check are in `checks/prompts/`.

The raw outputs are in [`runs/D_definitions/`](https://github.com/Dundalia/faithfulness-review/tree/main/runs/D_definitions). The claims and the verdicts of each check are in `claims_<term>.json`, one file per term. The collection of definitions, the groups and the checks are machine-made, so check a quote in the paper before citing it.
