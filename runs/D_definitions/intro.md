# Definitions of Faithfulness, Counterfactual and Monitorability

This page collects how the papers in the map define three terms: *faithfulness*, *counterfactual* and *monitorability*. It rests on 3,414 definitions quoted from 216 of the 217 entries the map held when the catalogue was built. The formal-framework papers added on 1 November 2026 have their own catalogue, in the Formal tab and on the [formal frameworks page](page.html?doc=formal). Each definition is quoted verbatim with its location in the paper, its kind (informal, formal, operational, criterion, failure mode or taxonomy) and its stance: proposed by the paper, or adopted or adapted from earlier work, in which case the row names the source (1,224 of the 1,225 borrowed rows do). The full set is [`definitions.csv`](https://github.com/Dundalia/faithfulness-review/blob/main/definitions.csv), one row per definition, and the Definitions tab of the dashboard filters it.

For each term, the definitions are grouped into clusters of meaning. Each section then traces who borrows which definition from whom, checks the borrowed definitions against their sources, and lists the points on which papers disagree. In the tables, text in quotation marks is copied from the paper and text without quotation marks is a gloss.

## Main findings

**Faithfulness has one common definition and nine relations that papers test, and the relations can disagree.** [Jacovi & Goldberg 2020](./#p=jacovi2020towards) define a faithful interpretation as one that "accurately represents the reasoning process behind the model’s prediction", and 79 rows from 55 papers cite them. Clusters 2 to 10 distinguish nine relations: what the trace discloses, whether the answer depends on the trace or agrees with it, whether an action matches what was said, whether the explanation lets an observer predict the model, whether a self-report matches the model's behavior or internal states, whether the marked inputs matter, whether the trace matches the internal computation, and whether a statement matches the model's belief. Some of them relax the common definition: [Baker et al. 2025](./#p=baker2025monitoring) require that "$z$ is a sufficient statistic for $v$" for one property $v$, and [Viteri et al. 2024](./#p=viteri2026markovian) prioritize "informativeness over full faithfulness". The relations can disagree on the same traces, and so can classifiers of a single relation: on identical data, the three hint-acknowledgment classifiers of [Young 2026a](./#p=young2026measuring) give overall faithfulness rates of 74.4%, 82.6% and 69.7%.

**Counterfactual definitions differ on four points.** They differ in what is varied, in whether the variant must change the output, in how a change in output becomes a causal claim, and in what the comparison judges: an explanation, the model's reliance on a feature, a self-report, a training target or a covert behavior. The section on counterfactuals lists ten tensions that follow from these four points, among them whether the variant must change the output and whether one flipped answer shows that the edit caused it.

**Monitorability has two competing definitions, and they divide on faithfulness.** 42 rows from 24 papers make monitorability a monitor's ability to predict a property of the model's behavior, and 69 rows from 34 papers make it depend on the trace verbalizing what drove the answer; 9 papers have rows in both clusters. [Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring) write that their notion "does not require a model’s externalized reasoning to faithfully reflect its internal reasoning process", and [Baker et al. 2025](./#p=baker2025monitoring) require faithfulness only with respect to the monitored property. [Jia et al. 2026](./#p=jia2026faithfulness), [Xiong et al. 2026](./#p=xiong2026monitorability) and [Anonymous 2026 (one draw)](./#p=anon2026one) state the requirement without that restriction.

**82% of the checks find a borrowed definition in its cited source.** Of the 804 borrowed rows that cite a source in the map, 803 have attribution checks, giving 1,095 row-source checks; the unchecked row is the cue-acknowledgment judge rubric of [Nguyen et al. 2026b](./#p=nguyen2026generalization), which cites [Meek et al. 2025](./#p=meek2025measuring). 898 find the definition in the source, as stated (647) or adapted (251). The other 197 are partial (122), renamed (43), support a different claim (13), are unsupported (15) or cite the wrong source (4). The checks cover 803 of the 804 eligible rows, so these are rates for the catalogue, not for a sample. In the faithfulness family, ten checks credit a source with a definition it does not give, three of them [Jacovi & Goldberg 2020](./#p=jacovi2020towards). In the counterfactual family, 6 of the 15 rows citing [Lanham et al. 2023](./#p=lanham2023measuring) fail the check.

## How this page was made

1. **First extraction.** `scripts/definitions.py excerpt` kept each section of a paper's full text that mentions one of the terms, without the reference list and most appendices, and Claude agents quoted the definitions in those sections: 1,197 definitions. The second pass showed that this missed definitions in methods sections and appendices.
2. **Second pass over the full texts.** Agents read every paper in full, appendices included, and added, corrected or dropped rows. The scope was widened to honesty, say-do consistency and self-report accuracy (faithfulness) and to scheming, covert actions, sabotage, sandbagging, deception, reward hacking and verbalized evaluation awareness (monitorability). Pure introspection is out of scope, and so is faithfulness to a source document unless the paper contrasts it explicitly with the notions above: GPT-6.1 Sol (through Codex) screened the 130 rows that a keyword search flagged and dropped 20. A sampled audit of 12 added rows found 3 errors (one row out of scope, two with the wrong stance or source), which are fixed (`checks/sample_audit_*.json`). Every one of the 3,414 quotes is found verbatim in the paper's text, up to whitespace and curly quotes.
3. **Attribution checks.** For 803 of the 804 borrowed rows whose source is in the map, an agent read the source and gave a verdict (1,095 row-source checks), recorded in the `attribution` column of the CSV. Sources outside the map were not checked, including Atanasova et al. (2023), cited by 18 counterfactual rows from 14 entries, 13 of them (from 10 entries) in the cluster on the counterfactual test of explanations.
4. **Sections.** One agent per term placed every row in exactly one cluster (the `cluster` column) and wrote the section, listing each factual claim it makes: 607 claims in all.
5. **Two independent checks of every claim.** GPT-6.1 Sol checked each claim against the papers' full texts and recomputed every count; 95 claims were wrong (38), overstated (52) or understated (5) and were replaced by the strongest statement the evidence supports. Claude Opus then checked every claim again, without seeing those verdicts, and corrected 54 more (19 wrong, 25 overstated, 10 understated); 43 of them had passed the first check. A last pass fixed references and counts that span sentences; the 15 sentences it reworded were checked a third time (3 corrected). On a sample of 31 claims, two independent runs of the first checker found 7 and 6 errors, 5 of them shared (`checks/calibration_*.json`), so a single check misses some errors; the second check reduces this risk without removing it. The prompts of every check are in `checks/prompts/`.

The raw outputs are in [`runs/D_definitions/`](https://github.com/Dundalia/faithfulness-review/tree/main/runs/D_definitions): the claims and the verdicts of each check are in `claims_<term>.json`. The extraction, the clusters and the checks are machine-made, so check a quote in the paper before citing it.
