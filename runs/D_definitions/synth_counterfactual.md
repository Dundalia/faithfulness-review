The counterfactual family holds 292 definition rows from 87 of the 217 papers. By kind, 109 rows are operational, 84 informal, 45 formal, 29 criteria, 19 taxonomies and 6 failure modes. By stance, 160 are proposed, 86 adopted and 46 adapted. Of the 292 rows, 163 define a counterfactual notion (162 of them carry the word in the term) and 129 define a companion notion, such as a validity criterion, an effect measure or a simulation metric. The second pass over the full texts added 163 rows and 14 papers, and 113 of the added rows are companion notions. The rows fall into the 12 clusters below. Clusters 2 and 3 test whether an observer’s "simulation matches the model’s actual output" on counterfactual inputs ([Chen et al. 2023](./#p=pmlr-v235-chen24bl)). Eight further clusters, 4 through 9, 11 and 12, concern self-predictions, input interventions, causal effect measures, validity criteria, explanation mention tests, reasoning interventions, model internals and covert behavior comparisons. In cluster 1, the counterfactual explanation is defined by what an edited input "would lead" the model to do ([Dehghanighobadi et al. 2025](./#p=dehghanighobadi2025can)); [Mayne et al. 2025](./#p=mayne2025llms) also map the model’s "decision boundary" to assess validity and minimality. In cluster 10, counterfactuals supply training data or reward signals, as in [Wang et al. 2023](./#p=wang2023scott). One row of cluster 12 runs no variant at all: in the counterfactual autorater of [Haskins et al. 2026](./#p=haskins2026training), a judge asks whether the reasoning "could ... plausibly have been produced in a scenario where no defect existed". The clusters differ on four points: what is varied; whether the variant must change the output; how a change in output becomes a causal claim; and what the comparison judges (an explanation, the model's reliance on a feature, a self-report, a training target or a covert behavior).

### 1. Counterfactual explanation: an edited input that would change the decision

This is the explainable-AI sense, which [Dehghanighobadi et al. 2025](./#p=dehghanighobadi2025can) and [Mayne et al. 2025](./#p=mayne2025llms) take from Wachter et al. 2017. The counterfactual is an alternative input that would lead the model to a different decision, and that input is itself the explanation. In 5 of the 10 papers the model writes the edit itself, which gives a self-generated counterfactual explanation (SCE): [Madsen et al. 2024](./#p=madsen2024self), [Dehghanighobadi et al. 2025](./#p=dehghanighobadi2025can), [Mayne et al. 2025](./#p=mayne2025llms), [Doi et al. 2025](./#p=doi2025investigating) and [Zeng et al. 2026](./#p=zeng2026evaluating). The edit is judged by validity (whether the model's own prediction on the edited input reaches the target; [Mayne et al. 2025](./#p=mayne2025llms) require a new context window), minimality and closeness. [Dehghanighobadi et al. 2025](./#p=dehghanighobadi2025can) separate validity from correctness, which asks whether the edit "actually evaluates to" the target by human judgment. Three papers make validity the condition for a trustworthy explanation: [Madsen et al. 2024](./#p=madsen2024self) ("If satisfied, the counterfactual is faithful"), [Doi et al. 2025](./#p=doi2025investigating) ("faithfulness can be evaluated by checking whether the generated counterfactuals indeed produce the prediction change") and [Mayne et al. 2025](./#p=mayne2025llms) ("Without validity, the explanation is a misleading representation of the model’s counterfactual behaviour"). Three rows use "counterfactual explanation" for a statement about how behavior would change, not for the edited input: [Agarwal et al. 2024](./#p=agarwal2024faithfulness), [Guo et al. 2026](./#p=guo2026introspective) and [Pres et al. 2026a](./#p=pres2026consistency). 28 rows from 10 papers (12 proposed, 13 adopted, 3 adapted).

> A counterfactual explanation $\mathbf{x}_{\text{CE}}$ is a modified version of the original input $\mathbf{x}$ that would lead the model to change its decision, that is, $f(\mathbf{x})\neq f(\mathbf{x}_{\text{CE}})$ .
>
> ([Dehghanighobadi et al. 2025](./#p=dehghanighobadi2025can), §3.1 (Step 2))

### 2. Counterfactual simulatability: an explanation that predicts behavior on other inputs

Here the counterfactual is the test input, not the explanation. An explanation given on $x$ is judged by whether an observer who reads it predicts the model's output on other inputs $x'$. [Chen et al. 2023](./#p=pmlr-v235-chen24bl) define the notion for free-text explanations and give two metrics, precision and generality, both computed over the counterfactuals the observer judges simulatable. The observer is a human in Chen et al.'s definition (approximated by GPT-4), "a human or model" in [Turpin et al. 2025](./#p=turpin2025teaching), LLM judges and a human study in [Hong & Roth 2026](./#p=hong2026llm), and a simulator model in [Hase & Potts 2026](./#p=hase2026counterfactual). What the observer predicts also varies: the model's answer ([Chen et al. 2023](./#p=pmlr-v235-chen24bl)), whether a new request is refused or complied with ([Pres et al. 2026b](./#p=pres2026selfctrl)), or whether a named prompt edit moves a behavior rate ([Karvonen et al. 2026](./#p=karvonen2026would)). [Hong & Roth 2026](./#p=hong2026llm) add a phase without the explanation and score the difference. The rows disagree on whether the notion measures faithfulness, plausibility or usefulness (Tensions, item 7). 34 rows from 15 papers (12 proposed, 14 adopted, 8 adapted).

> we propose to evaluate counterfactual simulatability of natural language explanations: whether an explanation can enable humans to precisely infer the model’s outputs on diverse counterfactuals of the explained input.
>
> ([Chen et al. 2023](./#p=pmlr-v235-chen24bl), Abstract)

### 3. The counterfactual set: which inputs simulatability is tested on

These rows define the inputs $x'$ on which an explanation from cluster 2 is tested. Six papers give five constructions:
- follow-up questions that an LLM writes from the explanation, instructed to write one "that you can confidently guess the robot’s answer to" ([Chen et al. 2023](./#p=pmlr-v235-chen24bl)), extended with exact paraphrases by [Chen et al. 2024](./#p=chen2025consistent) and used as the "non-pragmatic baseline" by [Hong & Roth 2026](./#p=hong2026llm);
- the input with a planted cue removed ([Hase & Potts 2026](./#p=hase2026counterfactual));
- LLM-generated related questions, rejection-sampled so that "the task model and simulator disagree on the correct answer" ([Hase & Potts 2026](./#p=hase2026counterfactual));
- four Gricean manipulations: presupposition flip, lexical substitution, scalar adjustment and contextualization ([Hong & Roth 2026](./#p=hong2026llm));
- real dataset rows close to the original in Hamming distance ([Mayne et al. 2026](./#p=mayne2026positivecasefaithfulnessllm), [Lewis-Lim et al. 2026](./#p=lewislim2026efficient)).

[Mayne et al. 2026](./#p=mayne2026positivecasefaithfulnessllm) state that "the validity of any evaluation hinges on this choice", restate three earlier designs as counterfactual sets (a biased prompt, random word insertion, single-concept edits) and name three pitfalls. ### 4. Counterfactual self-prediction: the model reports how it would behave under an edit

In this cluster the observer of cluster 2 is the model itself. It predicts its behavior under an edit using change judgments, flip rates, behavior probabilities or a "post-perturbation judge score" ([Zeng et al. 2026](./#p=zeng2026evaluating)), and these reports are scored against runs on the edited input. [Zeng et al. 2026](./#p=zeng2026evaluating) call this self-modeling, "an LLM’s ability to answer questions about its own behavior", and introduce a benchmark represented here by four tasks (Flip-Decision, Flip-Rate, Perturbation-Choice and Component-Attribution); their fifth row, Score-Prediction, describes a separate multi-turn BLOOM training task. [Karvonen et al. 2026](./#p=karvonen2026would) train counterfactual prediction as "causal self-report"; [Anonymous 2026 (introspective oversight)](./#p=anon2026trainingoversight) train an observer role sharing the assistant’s weights and reward its counterfactual behavior-probability predictions with a Brier score. [Zhang et al. 2026](./#p=zhang2026audit) score factor-importance self-reports against counterfactual audits and show that the audit rule decides whether truthful reporting pays: "report-dependent auditing creates a suppression incentive". [Mayne et al. 2025](./#p=mayne2025llms) name self-prediction as one of three necessary criteria for valid and minimal self-generated counterfactuals (cluster 1). Self-reports are checked against measured behavior using categorical accuracy and continuous scores: [Zeng et al. 2026](./#p=zeng2026evaluating) score scalar reports "by distance from the behavioral ground truth", while [Anonymous 2026 (introspective oversight)](./#p=anon2026trainingoversight) use a Brier score for behavior probabilities; this makes the cluster the counterfactual counterpart of the honesty and self-report rows in the other two families. 11 rows from 5 papers (6 proposed, 1 adopted, 4 adapted).

> In Flip-Decision, we test whether a model can predict whether a prompt perturbation will change its own aggregate behavior.
>
> ([Zeng et al. 2026](./#p=zeng2026evaluating), §2.4.1)

### 5. Input intervention: the same input with one cue or factor changed

This is the largest cluster by papers. A counterfactual is the original input with one specified element removed, inserted or replaced, with everything else held fixed, and a difference between the two outputs is read as that element's influence. The element is one of seven kinds:
- a planted hint, cue or biasing feature ([Turpin et al. 2023](./#p=turpin2023language), [Chua & Evans 2025](./#p=chua2025deepseek), [Ferreira et al. 2026](./#p=ferreira2026outofsight), [Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring), [Xiong et al. 2026](./#p=xiong2026monitorability), [Little 2026](./#p=little2026length), [Lewis-Lim et al. 2026](./#p=lewislim2026efficient), [Cheah et al. 2026](./#p=cheah2026training), [Alon et al. 2026](./#p=alon2026faithfulness), [Karvonen et al. 2026](./#p=karvonen2026would), [Guo et al. 2026](./#p=guo2026introspective), [Shiromani et al. 2026](./#p=shiromani2026necessary), [Bürger et al. 2026](./#p=burger2026chain), [Li 2026](./#p=li2026monitorability));
- a concept or structured factor ([Matton et al. 2025](./#p=matton2025walk), [Luo et al. 2026](./#p=luo2026removal), [Arcuschin et al. 2026](./#p=arcuschin2026biases), [Zhang et al. 2026](./#p=zhang2026audit));
- a protected attribute or user background ([Ferreira et al. 2025](./#p=ferreira2026truthful), [Pres et al. 2026a](./#p=pres2026consistency));
- the evidence passage ([Li et al. 2026](./#p=li2026when));
- an earlier turn of a conversation ([Zeng et al. 2026](./#p=zeng2026evaluating));
- a note tying a valued outcome to the answer ([Anonymous 2026 (introspective oversight)](./#p=anon2026trainingoversight));
- an edit that changes the correct answer ([Akter et al. 2025](./#p=akter2025causal), [Wang et al. 2026a](./#p=wang2026case)).

The rows cite three traditions for the design. [Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring) call it "standard in the bias and fairness literature", [Alon et al. 2026](./#p=alon2026faithfulness) take it from Kaushik et al. 2019, and [Ferreira et al. 2026](./#p=ferreira2026outofsight) note that it has "been widely used to assess chain-of-thought faithfulness" and monitorability. [Matton et al. 2025](./#p=matton2025walk) state the edit with Pearl's do-operator as a direct effect. 45 rows from 30 papers (19 proposed, 15 adopted, 11 adapted).

### 6. From paired runs to a causal claim: flip labels, total effects and potential outcomes

These rows state how runs on an original and a variant become a claim that the edit caused the outcome. They give four approaches to interpreting comparisons: paired flip labels or fractions, thresholded resampled rates, distributional effects and bounds on causal recall:
- a flip label on one pair ([Shiromani et al. 2026](./#p=shiromani2026necessary)), or a flip fraction over $n=3$ sampled replacements per factor by default ([Zhang et al. 2026](./#p=zhang2026audit));
- a resampled rate with thresholds ([Karvonen et al. 2026](./#p=karvonen2026would): positive when the cue raises the rate by at least 50 points, negative when it moves the rate by at most 15; [Zeng et al. 2026](./#p=zeng2026evaluating): three bands of flip fraction);
- Distributional or aggregate quantities include differences in target-answer rates ([Ferreira et al. 2026](./#p=ferreira2026outofsight), [Bürger et al. 2026](./#p=burger2026chain), [Anonymous 2026](./#p=anon2026chainofthought)), the hint-caused switch rate under the uniform-switching assumption ([Little 2026](./#p=little2026length)), and the acceptance-rate gap between positive and negative variants ([Arcuschin et al. 2026](./#p=arcuschin2026biases)). [Matton et al. 2025](./#p=matton2025walk) instead define "concept effects for a specific question": KL divergence between counterfactual and original answer distributions, averaged over alternative concept values.
- upper and lower bounds on the monitor's recall on "intervention-caused outcomes", $r_c=\Pr(Z(1)=1\mid Y(1)=1,Y(0)=0)$, under a single stable structural causal model with consistency and $p_1>p_0$ ([Anonymous 2026 (causal bounds)](./#p=anon2026improving)).

Three papers explain why a single flip is not enough. [Bürger et al. 2026](./#p=burger2026chain) reject the per-response criterion because it "ignores the stochastic and chaotic nature of LLM output sampling". [Angdembay et al. 2026](./#p=angdembay2026two) note that uncoupled sampling records "an observed conditional flip rather than a within-pair counterfactual". [Anonymous 2026 (causal bounds)](./#p=anon2026improving) show that separate arm samples do not identify "this cross-world coupling". [Shiromani et al. 2026](./#p=shiromani2026necessary) find that a third alternate candidate "flips 11-23% of verdicts", so their dependence label depends on which alternate defines the counterfactual. 23 rows from 13 papers (17 proposed, 2 adopted, 4 adapted).

>
> ([Ferreira et al. 2026](./#p=ferreira2026outofsight), §4)

### 7. Valid counterfactuals: isolation, quality and controls

These rows describe assumptions, validity checks, quality measures and controls for constructing counterfactual variants and interpreting their effects, including a locality measure used "as a soft filter" ([Han et al. 2026](./#p=han2026rfeval)). Thirteen papers contribute rows grouped here into seven categories of assumptions, quality measures, checks and controls:
- isolation: only the target changes ([Zhang et al. 2026](./#p=zhang2026audit), [Matton et al. 2025](./#p=matton2025walk), [Arcuschin et al. 2026](./#p=arcuschin2026biases), [Karvonen et al. 2026](./#p=karvonen2026would));
- quality of a generated edit: coherence and completeness ([Matton et al. 2025](./#p=matton2025walk)), four criteria for injected reasoning ([Han et al. 2026](./#p=han2026rfeval)), semantic opposition of a contrary hint ([Chuang et al. 2024](./#p=chuang2026faithlm)), label flip rate and textual similarity ([Hong & Roth 2026](./#p=hong2026llm)), and residual leakage of the original answer ([Li et al. 2026](./#p=li2026when));
- on-policy edits: text "the model could have plausibly produced" ([Macar et al. 2025](./#p=macar2025thought));
- informativeness: the injected reasoning must oppose the model's baseline stance ([Han et al. 2026](./#p=han2026rfeval));
- executability: both plans must succeed under forced execution ([Fareed 2026](./#p=fareed2026plan));
- controls: semantics-preserving null edits ([Akter et al. 2025](./#p=akter2025causal)) and matched swaps that keep the claim fixed ([Ananthula & Kumarappan 2026](./#p=ananthula2026decodable));
- for a parameter edit, efficacy and specificity of unlearning ([Tutek et al. 2025](./#p=tutek2025measuring)).

Two papers report how much their checks remove: the isolation check of [Arcuschin et al. 2026](./#p=arcuschin2026biases) "removed $42$ % of candidate concepts", and [Han et al. 2026](./#p=han2026rfeval) "started with 8,499 instances and removed 1,313". 25 rows from 13 papers (22 proposed, 2 adopted, 1 adapted).

### 8. The counterfactual test of explanations: does the explanation mention the edit?

These rows use the same counterfactual as cluster 5, an input edit, but they judge the explanation, not the model's reliance on the edited element. The procedure is that of Atanasova et al. 2023, which 13 of the 17 rows cite: words are inserted into the input, and if the prediction changes while the explanation of the edited instance does not mention the inserted words, the explanation counts as unfaithful. Siegel et al. 2024, cited by 8 rows, replace the binary outcome with a correlation between mentions and the shift in the label distribution (the Correlational Counterfactual Test, CCT). Neither paper is in the map. ### 10. Counterfactual training pairs

These rows use counterfactuals as training data or as a reward signal, not as a test. ### 12. Counterfactual baselines for scheming, obfuscation and dishonesty

In these rows the counterfactual condition is the evidence that a behavior is strategic, hidden or dishonest, in settings where a single trajectory does not show it. The rows cover six behaviors:
- sandbagging: the environment's incentive is reversed, and the behavior is compared across the two conditions ([Schoen et al. 2025](./#p=schoen2025stress));
- sabotage: the same coding task is framed as for the model itself or for its successor, and "o4-mini SAB underperforms on its successor’s code at a significantly higher rate than on its own" ([Schoen et al. 2025](./#p=schoen2025stress));
- evaluation awareness: awareness is injected into or removed from the CoT to measure its "causal effect ... on the pursuit of covert actions" ([Schoen et al. 2025](./#p=schoen2025stress));
- steganographic reliance: an ordinary RL agent that "learns to evade the monitor to secretly encode information" shows a large gap between the original and the "counterfactual" reward when only the applicant's nationality is changed ([Farquhar et al. 2025](./#p=farquhar2025mona));
- obfuscated denial: a judge asks whether reasoning "could ... plausibly have been produced in a scenario where no defect existed"; it labels "93.8% of behavior-only CoTs as impossible without the defect, compared to only 38.4% for the monitor-aware model" ([Haskins et al. 2026](./#p=haskins2026training));
- dishonesty: a neutral variant of each query verifies what the model knows before honesty is scored under pressure ([Wang et al. 2025b](./#p=wang2025evaluating)); biased and unbiased prompts verify "whether specific factors actually influenced behavior" for an honest-persona reviewer ([Dietz et al. 2026](./#p=dietz2026split)); a persona system prompt tests whether denial of tampering reflects "a fixed “safe and reliable assistant” persona" ([Nguyen et al. 2026](./#p=nguyen2026can)).

Six kinds of drift appear as the definitions are passed on.

1. **Simulatability changes status.** [Chen et al. 2023](./#p=pmlr-v235-chen24bl) ask whether an explanation lets humans "precisely infer the model’s outputs on diverse counterfactuals of the explained input", and write that "simulatability is a special case of faithfulness" and "a faithful explanation is not necessarily simulatable". [Turpin et al. 2023](./#p=turpin2023language) call it "The counterfactual simulatability framework of explanation faithfulness". In [Turpin et al. 2025](./#p=turpin2025teaching) it becomes a biconditional: "an explanation is faithful if and only if the explanation allows a human or model to accurately predict how the model will behave on other relevant inputs". [Hong & Roth 2026](./#p=hong2026llm) change what is predicted, to "how a model’s prediction would change on counterfactual inputs", and call the property usefulness. [Karvonen et al. 2026](./#p=karvonen2026would) change the counterfactuals: Chen et al. are said to argue that "a good explanation should help an observer predict behavior under counterfactual edits". Three things shift along the chain: the observer goes from humans to "a human or model", the counterfactuals go from LLM-written related questions to edits, and the notion goes from a special case of faithfulness to a necessary and sufficient condition for it. The attribution check records the biconditional as an adaptation of Chen et al.
2. **The counterfactual explanation acquires a target, changes author and loses the target again.** In [Dehghanighobadi et al. 2025](./#p=dehghanighobadi2025can), after Wachter et al., it is an input that "would lead the model to change its decision". [Mayne et al. 2025](./#p=mayne2025llms) tie it to "a specific alternative output", and [Madsen et al. 2024](./#p=madsen2024self) to "the opposite prediction". The edit is then no longer found by an optimizer: [Dehghanighobadi et al. 2025](./#p=dehghanighobadi2025can) "ask the model itself to generate the counterfactual explanation", and [Doi et al. 2025](./#p=doi2025investigating) add "editing-distance constraints specified in a prompt". [Zeng et al. 2026](./#p=zeng2026evaluating) recast it as "the propose-and-verify form of Edit-Proposal" and drop the target label: the edit counts as a flip if "the fraction of resamples that differ from baseline exceeds $0.5$".
3. **The counterfactual test widens.** [Parcalabescu & Frank 2023](./#p=parcalabescu2023measuring) describe Atanasova et al. as training "a helper model to insert words into the LLM input". [Wang & Atanasova 2025](./#p=wang2025selfcritique) generate the inserted modifiers with GPT-4o. [Gema et al. 2026](./#p=gema2026chainofthought) describe counterfactual approaches as testing explanations "through input edits, reconstruction, or output-distribution shifts", [Bhan et al. 2025](./#p=bhan2025did) as using "auxiliary models to generate counterfactual texts designed to change the LLM outcome", and [Cheah et al. 2026](./#p=cheah2026training) as using "controlled interventions, such as the introduction of biasing hints". By this point the label "counterfactual test" also covers the hint pairs that [Turpin et al. 2023](./#p=turpin2023language) had filed under counterfactual simulatability. [Mayne et al. 2026](./#p=mayne2026positivecasefaithfulnessllm) restate word insertion as a counterfactual set and list it among "Irrelevant or incoherent perturbations".
4. **Borrowers add the word.** [Lanham et al. 2023](./#p=lanham2023measuring) use "counterfactual" once in the text, for the cited work of Madaan & Yazdanbakhsh. Of the 15 rows that borrow from Lanham et al., 6 rows from 5 papers ([Admoni et al. 2025](./#p=admoni2026aligning), [Anonymous 2026 (GRACE)](./#p=anon2026grace), [Li et al. 2025a](./#p=li2025drift), [Madsen et al. 2024](./#p=madsen2024self), [Shen et al. 2026](./#p=shen2026detecting)) call its tests counterfactual in the quoted passage, and [Parekh & Goyal 2026](./#p=parekh2026drop) name truncation "prefix counterfactuals". [Chen et al. 2025](./#p=chen2025reasoning) use the word once, for Paul et al., yet [Alon et al. 2026](./#p=alon2026faithfulness) write of the same hint-flip filter "we regard this as a counterfactual". [Wiegreffe et al. 2021](./#p=wiegreffe2021measuring) never use the word, and [Admoni et al. 2025](./#p=admoni2026aligning) list them among works that "apply counterfactual interventions to input features".
5. **The cue-removal label tightens in one line and loosens in another.** [Hase & Potts 2026](./#p=hase2026counterfactual) "primarily use greedy decoding" for the original and counterfactual inputs. [Karvonen et al. 2026](./#p=karvonen2026would) take their cue types but label with 10 samples per condition, counting a case positive only if "the cue raises the rate of the cue-consistent answer by at least 50pp". In the other direction, [Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring) hold that "we cannot label individual samples as having been causally influenced" unless the base rate is zero, while [Ferreira et al. 2026](./#p=ferreira2026outofsight), who cite Guan et al., use "the counterfactual prediction without the hint" to get "a per-example target label".
6. **Lanham et al.'s tests are re-parameterized and extended.** [Lanham et al. 2023](./#p=lanham2023measuring) truncate the CoT "after each step (here, each sentence)"; [Anonymous 2026 (GRACE)](./#p=anon2026grace) truncate "at 25/50/75% token levels". [Anonymous 2026 (CRISP)](./#p=anon2026crisp) have a generator write a chain with "exactly ONE decisive error". [Parekh & Goyal 2026](./#p=parekh2026drop) decode greedily to find a commitment point. [Shen et al. 2026](./#p=shen2026detecting) list five tests under Lanham et al., two of which (Option Shuffling and Removing Steps) Lanham et al. do not run (see the attribution check).

### Tensions

1. **Must the variant change the output?**
   - A changed decision defines counterfactual explanations in [Dehghanighobadi et al. 2025](./#p=dehghanighobadi2025can) and [Mayne et al. 2025](./#p=mayne2025llms). [Wang & Atanasova 2025](./#p=wang2025selfcritique) retain prediction-changing instances, and [Alon et al. 2026](./#p=alon2026faithfulness) call a pair counterfactual when a₀ ≠ a₁ = hint. [Han et al. 2026](./#p=han2026rfeval) instead impose a "contrast precondition": injected reasoning must oppose the baseline reasoning stance. Whether the model's subsequent reasoning or answer changes is evaluated separately, and unchanged outputs remain possible.
   - The output must not change: [Zaman & Srivastava 2025a](./#p=zaman2025causal) build knowledge edits that "yield the same answer but are based on different reasoning"; [Jain & Wallace 2019](./#p=jain2019attention) define adversarial attention by "an equivalent prediction"; [Akter et al. 2025](./#p=akter2025causal) use null interventions, "semantics-preserving transformations that should not change the answer", and count a flip on them as spurious; [Shiromani et al. 2026](./#p=shiromani2026necessary) keep only traces that end "in the same final answer type".
   - The edit defines the counterfactual and the flip is measured: [Zeng et al. 2026](./#p=zeng2026evaluating) define a perturbation as "a modification to a prompt that may or may not change the model’s answer", and [Matton et al. 2025](./#p=matton2025walk), [Zhang et al. 2026](./#p=zhang2026audit) and [Cheah et al. 2026](./#p=cheah2026training) define the counterfactual by the edit alone.

   For the first group a non-flipping edit is not a counterfactual, for [Akter et al. 2025](./#p=akter2025causal) a flipping null edit is an error, and for the third group either outcome is data.
2. **Remove the cue or swap it?** [Hase & Potts 2026](./#p=hase2026counterfactual) ("The counterfactual input is the input without the cue."), [Guo et al. 2026](./#p=guo2026introspective) ($x_{\setminus C}$), [Karvonen et al. 2026](./#p=karvonen2026would) ("Ground truth comes from removing the cue"), [Ferreira et al. 2026](./#p=ferreira2026outofsight), [Ferreira et al. 2025](./#p=ferreira2026truthful) and [Bürger et al. 2026](./#p=burger2026chain) compare against the input without the cue. [Shiromani et al. 2026](./#p=shiromani2026necessary) reject that comparison: "Comparing W to a no-hint prompt would confound the hint’s content with its presence". For them, "the hint caused the answer" means that the answer changes when $h$ is swapped for $h'$, and relabeling with a third candidate "flips 11-23% of verdicts". [Zhang et al. 2026](./#p=zhang2026audit) and [Arcuschin et al. 2026](./#p=arcuschin2026biases) also replace a value instead of deleting it. [Li 2026](./#p=li2026monitorability) and [Little 2026](./#p=little2026length) ask for both no-hint and alternate-hint runs, and [Matton et al. 2025](./#p=matton2025walk) use both removal and value-change counterfactuals while warning that correlated concepts are "especially a risk when we use removal-based counterfactuals". A given answer can count as caused by a hint under removal and not under a swap.
3. **Does one flip show that the edit caused the answer?**
   - Yes, per instance: [Alon et al. 2026](./#p=alon2026faithfulness) treat each flip "as evidence that the hint influenced the model’s decision making"; [Ferreira et al. 2025](./#p=ferreira2026truthful) "regard difference in predictions ... as evidence that the protected feature ... exerts causal influence"; [Ferreira et al. 2026](./#p=ferreira2026outofsight) take the greedy prediction without the hint as "a per-example target label"; [Cheah et al. 2026](./#p=cheah2026training) hold that "any change in behavior can be attributed to" the intervention.
   - No: [Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring) write that unless the base rate is zero "we cannot label individual samples as having been causally influenced"; [Bürger et al. 2026](./#p=burger2026chain) reject the per-response flip criterion; [Angdembay et al. 2026](./#p=angdembay2026two) call their own label "an observed conditional flip rather than a within-pair counterfactual"; [Anonymous 2026 (causal bounds)](./#p=anon2026improving) show that "pairing unrelated control and intervention completions does not identify the coupling"; [Li 2026](./#p=li2026monitorability) state that their label "records answer–hint agreement, not a verified causal hint effect".
   - In between: [Karvonen et al. 2026](./#p=karvonen2026would) resample and label a claim only when the rate moves by at least 50 points or at most 15; [Zeng et al. 2026](./#p=zeng2026evaluating) count a flip when more than half of $K$ resamples differ.

   The second group also disagrees about what the aggregate shows. [Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring) write that the total effect "lower bounds the causal influence rate" and score monitors against it; [Anonymous 2026 (causal bounds)](./#p=anon2026improving) show that the resulting score, $\mathrm{TPR}_{mc}$, "upper-bounds the monitor’s recall on intervention-caused outcomes". [Shen et al. 2026](./#p=shen2026detecting) write that CoT intervention analysis "does not directly determine the faithfulness of a specific generated trace", yet run five counterfactual tests as baselines for detecting unfaithfulness in individual traces.
4. **Isolate one factor, or vary more than one?** [Karvonen et al. 2026](./#p=karvonen2026would) exclude a counterfactual that is "confounded, editing several variables at once"; [Arcuschin et al. 2026](./#p=arcuschin2026biases) rate a pair 1, "Not a valid counterfactual", for "Multiple or major changes"; [Matton et al. 2025](./#p=matton2025walk) assume concepts can each "be changed without affecting other concepts"; [Zhang et al. 2026](./#p=zhang2026audit) require edits that "leave the other factors fixed". [Mayne et al. 2026](./#p=mayne2026positivecasefaithfulnessllm) count single-factor variation as a pitfall: "Most prior approaches vary only a single concept at a time (Matton et al., 2025; Siegel et al., 2024). This fails to test whether the explanations capture the complex, non-linear concept interactions". Their counterfactual set holds real rows that differ in up to two features. The split follows the purpose: the first four papers attribute an effect to one factor, while Mayne et al. test whether an explanation predicts behavior across a region.
5. **Edit the reasoning by hand, or resample it?** The Adding Mistakes test of [Lanham et al. 2023](./#p=lanham2023measuring) has "a language model add a mistake somewhere in the original CoT"; [Han et al. 2026](./#p=han2026rfeval) insert reasoning written by o3, and [Khanzadeh 2026](./#p=khanzadeh2026project) a step rewritten by a critic model. [Macar et al. 2025](./#p=macar2025thought) describe prior work, Lanham et al. included, as "inserting or replacing sentences with handwritten text", define an intervention as off-policy "if the model would not plausibly produce it itself (e.g., a handwritten sentence or one taken from a different context)" and report that "artificial and off-policy edits to CoT common in prior literature generally produce small and unstable changes in behavior, while on-policy resampling produces larger and more reliable effects". [Madsen et al. 2024](./#p=madsen2024self) raise a related doubt about Lanham et al.'s edited CoTs: "injecting false facts may create out-of-distribution results or be interpreted as typos by the LLM, thus it’s unclear if this method is completely valid". The papers also differ on what happens after the edit. [Khanzadeh 2026](./#p=khanzadeh2026project) re-execute the agent "from the point of intervention", so later steps are re-sampled; [Macar et al. 2025](./#p=macar2025thought) argue that their own counterfactual importance "misses resilience" and measure importance on rollouts in which the sentence's content never reappears.
6. **Which inputs count as counterfactuals for simulatability?** [Chen et al. 2023](./#p=pmlr-v235-chen24bl) have an LLM write follow-up questions "that you can confidently guess the robot’s answer to based on its answer to the starter question". [Hase & Potts 2026](./#p=hase2026counterfactual) rejection-sample generated questions "to obtain counterfactuals where the task model and simulator disagree on the correct answer". [Mayne et al. 2026](./#p=mayne2026positivecasefaithfulnessllm) and [Lewis-Lim et al. 2026](./#p=lewislim2026efficient) use "similar real inputs from the same dataset rather than synthetic perturbations", and [Hong & Roth 2026](./#p=hong2026llm) use Gricean manipulations. [Mayne et al. 2026](./#p=mayne2026positivecasefaithfulnessllm) name the "unconstrained LLM-generated edits (Chen et al., 2024)" among approaches that "often produce counterfactuals that are irrelevant or drift from the natural data distribution". Chen et al.'s generator writes questions it can answer from the explanation, while Hase & Potts keep questions on which the simulator and the task model disagree. Precision is averaged over the chosen set, so each score is defined relative to its set.
7. **What does counterfactual simulatability measure?**
   - Faithfulness: [Turpin et al. 2023](./#p=turpin2023language) ("framework of explanation faithfulness"), [Turpin et al. 2025](./#p=turpin2025teaching) (the "if and only if"), [Arcuschin et al. 2025](./#p=arcuschin2025chain) ("measure faithfulness through the framework of counterfactual simulatability"), [Mayne et al. 2026](./#p=mayne2026positivecasefaithfulnessllm) ("a faithful explanation ... should help predict its behavior across $C(x)$") and [Karvonen et al. 2026](./#p=karvonen2026would) ("A faithful explanation should tell a reader what would change the behavior").
   - A special case of faithfulness that is not necessary for it: [Chen et al. 2023](./#p=pmlr-v235-chen24bl) ("a faithful explanation is not necessarily simulatable"). [Hong & Roth 2026](./#p=hong2026llm) repeat this and study simulatability "as a way to capture the usefulness of explanations", adding that "explanations can improve simulatability while still being post-hoc or persuasive rather than diagnostic".
   - Plausibility: [Agarwal et al. 2024](./#p=agarwal2024faithfulness) introduce Chen et al.'s metrics after writing that "most evaluations of self-explanations focus on plausibility".

   The biconditional of [Turpin et al. 2025](./#p=turpin2025teaching) makes simulatability necessary for faithfulness, and [Chen et al. 2023](./#p=pmlr-v235-chen24bl) state that it is not. [Karvonen et al. 2026](./#p=karvonen2026would) also restrict what an explanation is: "a behavioral claim testable by counterfactual experiments, not an explanation of the model’s internal computation". The [faithfulness](#faithfulness) section covers the faithfulness side.
8. **Does the counterfactual test measure faithfulness?**
   - Yes: [Wang & Atanasova 2025](./#p=wang2025selfcritique), [Alon et al. 2026](./#p=alon2026faithfulness), [Cheah et al. 2026](./#p=cheah2026training), [Zaman & Srivastava 2025b](./#p=zaman2025chainofthought) and [Gema et al. 2026](./#p=gema2026chainofthought) use it as a test of explanation faithfulness.
   - Qualified: [Parcalabescu & Frank 2023](./#p=parcalabescu2023measuring) characterize all the tests they review, Counterfactual Edits included, "as self-consistency tests instead"; [Baker et al. 2025](./#p=baker2025monitoring) credit them (their [76]) with the argument that a test relying on "executing changes to the model’s input and observing changes to the prediction actually measures self-consistency rather than faithfulness". [Bhan et al. 2025](./#p=bhan2025did) name two limitations, that "the model might base its new prediction on another part of the input text after intervention" and that the methods "treat the model as a black box by only examining input-output relationships", which "departs from the commonly adopted definition of explanation faithfulness"; they still run the test as a comparison. [Matton et al. 2025](./#p=matton2025walk) object that it judges "explanations given to the counterfactual questions, which may not reflect the faithfulness of the LLM in response to the original questions". [Mayne et al. 2026](./#p=mayne2026positivecasefaithfulnessllm) list random word insertions among perturbations that "often produce counterfactuals that are irrelevant or drift from the natural data distribution". [Admoni et al. 2025](./#p=admoni2026aligning) describe the correlational version as "population-level".
9. **Is the answer's dependence on the reasoning a measure of faithfulness?** [Akter et al. 2025](./#p=akter2025causal) call Counterfactual Sensitivity "a formal, causally-motivated measure of faithfulness" and write that "Higher COS indicates greater faithfulness"; [Shen et al. 2026](./#p=shen2026detecting) read an unchanged answer after substantial perturbation as "suggesting potential unfaithfulness"; [Somov et al. 2026](./#p=somov2026breaking) write that "A faithful model should shift its output accordingly". [Baker et al. 2025](./#p=baker2025monitoring) (App. G.2) state that their "definition of faithfulness does not imply any expectations of causality", that causal CoTs "are not a necessary condition for monitorability", and that "intervention-based proxies for measuring causality are insufficient measures for faithfulness", because a change after a CoT edit "will not reveal how much of the complete reasoning is encapsulated in the CoT". They still call causal metrics "valuable and worth measuring", since "steerability is a sufficient condition for faithfulness". On Baker et al.'s account, strong steerability raises confidence in faithfulness, but a change after an edit does not show how much of the reasoning the CoT contains; COS and CS read the size of the response as the degree of faithfulness.
10. **Attention counterfactuals.** [Jain & Wallace 2019](./#p=jain2019attention) take the existence of attention weights "maximally distinct from $\hat{\alpha}$ but that nonetheless yields an equivalent prediction" as evidence against attention as explanation. [Wiegreffe & Pinter 2019](./#p=wiegreffe2019attention) accept the counterfactual framework "as a concept" but object that "due to the per-instance nature of the demonstration and the fact that model parameters have not been learned or manipulated directly, Jain and Wallace have not shown the existence of an adversarial model that produces the claimed adversarial distributions". On their reading, an alternative attention distribution counts as a counterfactual only if a trained model produces it.
