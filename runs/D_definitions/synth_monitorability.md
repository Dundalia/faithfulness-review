The monitorability section of this page holds 1,167 definitions from 108 papers. Each definition has a kind and a stance. The kind says what the definition gives: informal (a definition in words), formal (a mathematical definition), operational (a way to measure or test the notion), criterion (a necessary or sufficient condition for the notion, or an assumption about it), failure mode (a way the notion can fail) or taxonomy (a classification into types). The stance says where the definition comes from: proposed (the paper's own), adopted (taken unchanged from earlier work) or adapted (taken from earlier work and changed). By kind, the 1,167 definitions are 448 operational, 268 informal, 133 failure modes, 119 criteria, 105 taxonomies and 94 formal; by stance, 785 are proposed, 257 adopted and 125 adapted.

The definitions were collected in two rounds. The first found 328 definitions in 82 papers; the second, which read the papers' full texts, raised the count to 1,167: 327 of the earlier definitions remain, one moved to the faithfulness section, and 840 are new. 127 definitions, from 47 papers, define monitorability itself or a direct measure of it (in 109 of them the defined term contains the word monitorability, and in another 14 the word monitorable or unmonitorable). The other 1,040 define related notions: monitors and their scores, failure modes, and the behaviors monitors look for (deception, scheming, reward hacking, evaluation awareness).

Two definitions of monitorability compete. 42 definitions from 24 papers make monitorability a monitor's ability to predict a property of the model's behavior, and 69 definitions from 34 papers make it depend on whether the reasoning trace states (verbalizes) what drove the answer; 9 papers give definitions of both types. The definitions are sorted by meaning into the 18 groups below, and each definition belongs to exactly one group. The groups run in this order: what monitorability is (groups 1 and 2); how it is measured (metrics, evaluation designs); what makes a trace readable (necessity, legibility) and how it stops being readable (encoded reasoning, obfuscation, evasion of the monitor); the monitor itself (monitors, the prompts and rules behind their verdicts, failures of the monitor, the model's own reports); the behaviors monitors target (deception, scheming, reward hacking, evaluation awareness); and the cost of keeping a model monitorable.

Each group below starts with its number of definitions and papers and its counts by kind and by stance. A short description, one quoted definition and a table of definitions follow. In the tables, text in quotation marks is copied from the paper. Text without quotation marks is not a quote: it is a formula restating the paper's mathematics, a short paraphrase, or both (all text in italics is paraphrase). The line after each table lists the group's remaining definitions, with the number from each paper in parentheses.

### 1. Monitorability as a monitor's ability to predict a property of the model's behavior

42 definitions from 24 papers. Kinds: 24 informal, 13 criteria, 3 formal, 1 operational, 1 taxonomy. Stances: 21 proposed, 17 adopted, 4 adapted.

These definitions make monitorability a relation among three things: a model, a target property of its behavior and a reader. A model is monitorable for a property to the degree that a monitor, reading what the model exposes, predicts that property. The reader is part of the definition: [Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring) call monitorability "a two-sided property", [Algaba et al. 2026](./#p=algaba2026reasoning) "a joint property of the display and the reader" (the display is what the reader is shown), and [Anonymous 2026 (CoT reliance)](./#p=anon2026chainofthought) "a property of both the task model and monitor". Seven definitions from seven papers distinguish monitorability from faithfulness or from a complete reconstruction of the reasoning: [Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring), [Baker et al. 2025](./#p=baker2025monitoring), [Korbak et al. 2025](./#p=korbak2025chain), [MacDermott et al. 2025](./#p=macdermott2025reasoning), [Kaufmann et al. 2026](./#p=kaufmann2026aligned), [Lewis-Lim et al. 2026](./#p=lewislim2026efficient) and [Young 2026](./#p=young2026why). [Emmons et al. 2025 (necessity)](./#p=emmons2025chain) do not draw this contrast in their definition, but they argue in the same paper that "the key property is not faithfulness but monitorability". An eighth definition, also in [Baker et al. 2025](./#p=baker2025monitoring), rejects a separate requirement: it says that "causal CoTs" are unnecessary for monitorability, because a trace may faithfully describe reasoning without causally determining the output. The group also holds the premise of plan-based monitoring in [Fareed 2026](./#p=fareed2026plan), "that reading the plan tells an overseer which irreversible actions the model will take". An earlier version of this page put it in the necessity group (group 5); an independent check of that version by another model (GPT-6.1 Sol) placed it here, since it defines usefulness to an overseer rather than necessity.

> We develop evaluations for monitorability, which we define as a monitor’s ability to accurately predict a specified property of an agent’s actions, e.g. whether the model is acting or will act harmfully.
>
> ([Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring), §2)

**Formal versions of these definitions.**

- [Anonymous 2026 (implementation)](./#p=anon2026implementation), Informal Def. 1: a property $P$ of an actor $A$ (the monitored model) is monitorable on a task distribution $D_t$ with respect to a monitor $M$ if the monitor's output on a transcript approximately matches the property with high probability. In symbols, $P(A,t,\pi)\approx M(\pi)$ with high probability over tasks $t\sim D_t$ and transcripts $\pi\sim A(t)$ that the actor produces on them.
- [Anwar et al. 2026 (information theory)](./#p=anwar2026analyzing), Def. 3.6 and Props. 3.7 and 3.10: the uplift from reading the CoT $Z$ is the accuracy gain of the Bayes-optimal CoT monitor (the most accurate possible monitor that reads the prompt and the CoT) over the Bayes-optimal prompt-only monitor, $\delta:=\alpha_{\text{CoT}}-\alpha_{\text{Prompt}}$. The uplift is bounded by the conditional mutual information $I(Y;Z\mid X)$, the information that the CoT carries about the monitored attribute $Y$ beyond what the prompt $X$ gives: $0\le\delta\le\sqrt{2I(Y;Z\mid X)}$. For an attribute given by a stochastic map of the output $O$ and prompt $X$, $Y=g(O,X)$, a positive uplift requires that the CoT carry some information about the output beyond the prompt: $\delta>0$ requires $I(O;Z\mid X)>0$.

### 2. Monitorability as a trace that states, faithfully and completely, what drove the answer

69 definitions from 34 papers. Kinds: 24 criteria, 14 operational, 14 informal, 8 formal, 5 failure modes, 4 taxonomies. Stances: 51 proposed, 16 adopted, 2 adapted.

These definitions make monitorability depend on what the trace states: monitoring presupposes that the CoT states the factors and steps that produced the answer, and monitorability falls when it does not. They differ on how much the trace must state. [Bürger et al. 2026](./#p=burger2026chain) require only "the key influential factors and reasoning steps that shaped the answer" and [Kaufmann et al. 2026](./#p=kaufmann2026aligned) only "the specific computations the monitor needs to detect", while [Xiong et al. 2026](./#p=xiong2026monitorability) require traces that "faithfully reflect the model’s internal decision-making path" and [Jia et al. 2026](./#p=jia2026faithfulness) a trace that "faithfully reflects the computation that produces the final answer". Three definitions score monitorability by how often the trace states something (a verbalization rate) rather than by a monitor's predictions. They come from experiments with an inserted cue or hint, such as a suggested answer added to the prompt. [Meek et al. 2025](./#p=meek2025measuring) average a cue-acknowledgment rate (how often the trace mentions the cue), which they call faithfulness, and a verbosity rate; [Occhipinti et al. 2026](./#p=occhipinti2026probing) count every trace where the hint's influence is visible; and [Nguyen et al. 2026](./#p=nguyen2026generalization) restate Occhipinti's measure, noting that it is reported "over only the traces that were unfaithful at baseline".

> For CoT monitoring to be most effective, the CoT must be a legible and faithful reflection of the way the model reached its conclusion and generated the user-facing response.
>
> ([Chen et al. 2025](./#p=chen2025reasoning), §1)

### 3. Metrics that score monitorability

98 definitions from 35 papers. Kinds: 58 operational, 35 formal, 4 criteria, 1 informal. Stances: 67 proposed, 19 adopted, 12 adapted.

These definitions include measures that compare a monitor's verdict $Z$ with a label $Y$ for the target property. The comparison is summarized by g-mean² (the true-positive rate times the true-negative rate, defined below), AUROC (the area under the ROC curve: the probability that the monitor scores a random positive case above a random negative one), binned mean squared error, recall at a fixed false-positive rate, or accuracy. The group also holds indirect proxies, such as the "proxy objective" $I(O;Z\mid X)$ proposed by [Anwar et al. 2026 (information theory)](./#p=anwar2026analyzing), in whose notation $Z$ is the CoT, $X$ the prompt and $O$ the output; $I(O;Z\mid X)$ is the information that the CoT carries about the output beyond what the prompt gives. In intervention evaluations, the model runs with and without a controlled change to its input or environment (the intervention arm and the control arm), and the monitor must detect when the change altered the model's reasoning and behavior. For these evaluations, [Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring) replace the true-positive rate by a minimal-criterion rate. It credits the monitor only up to $R_{\text{effect}}$, the share of positive cases that the intervention must have caused, and six papers reuse that rate: [Anonymous 2026 (CoT reliance)](./#p=anon2026chainofthought), [Anonymous 2026 (improving monitorability)](./#p=anon2026improving), [Anonymous 2026 (reward misspecification)](./#p=anon2026reward), [Lewis-Lim et al. 2026](./#p=lewislim2026efficient), [Wang et al. 2026](./#p=wang2026monitorbench) and [Xiong et al. 2026](./#p=xiong2026monitorability). [Anonymous 2026 (improving monitorability)](./#p=anon2026improving) define causal recall, the share of the cases in which the intervention caused the behavior that the monitor flags. They prove (Theorem 1) that, under their Assumption 1, the minimal-criterion rate is the upper end of the interval that the observed arms leave for causal recall. So the rate bounds causal recall from above but "does not generally identify it" (the bounds collapse to the observed recall when $p_0=0$, that is, when the target outcome never occurs in the control arm). [Bürger et al. 2026](./#p=burger2026chain) keep the same form but put the CoT's articulation rate (how often the CoT states the influence) where the monitor's flag rate was.

> A natural choice is the geometric mean of sensitivity (true positive rate) and specificity (true negative rate), $\text{g-mean}=\sqrt{\mathrm{TPR}\cdot\mathrm{TNR}}$ [25]. We square this quantity to more sharply penalize failures, yielding a monitorability measure which is both more conservative and more immediately interpretable
>
> ([Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring), §2.1)

**Formal versions of these metrics.**

- [Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring) multiply the true-positive rate (TPR, the share of positive cases the monitor flags) by the true-negative rate (TNR, the share of negative cases it does not flag): $\text{g-mean}^2=\mathrm{TPR}\cdot\mathrm{TNR}$. In the intervention setting, $X=1$ marks the intervention arm and $X=0$ the control arm, $Y=1$ the target behavior and $Z=1$ a monitor flag. The minimal-criterion true-positive rate divides the monitor's flag rate on positive cases in the intervention arm by $R_{\text{effect}}$ and caps the result at 1. Here $R_{\text{effect}}$ is the total effect $\mathrm{TE}$ of the intervention (the difference in the rate of the target behavior between the intervention arm and the control arm) divided by the rate of the behavior in the intervention arm: $\mathrm{TPR}_{\mathrm{mc}}=\min\{1,\Pr(Z=1\mid X=1,Y=1)/R_{\text{effect}}\}$ with $R_{\text{effect}}=\mathrm{TE}/\Pr(Y=1\mid X=1)$. The score multiplies this rate by the geometric mean of the true-negative rates in the two arms: $\text{g-mean}^2=\mathrm{TPR}_{\mathrm{mc}}\sqrt{\mathrm{TNR}_{X=1}\,\mathrm{TNR}_{X=0}}$.
- [Anonymous 2026 (improving monitorability)](./#p=anon2026improving) define causal recall with potential outcomes: $Y(1)$ and $Y(0)$ are the behavior the model would show with and without the intervention, and $Z(1)$ is the monitor's verdict with it. Causal recall is the probability of a flag among the cases in which the intervention caused the behavior: $r_c:=\Pr(Z(1)=1\mid U=1)$ with $U:=\mathbf{1}\{Y(1)=1,Y(0)=0\}$. With $R=(p_1-p_0)/p_1$, where $p_1$ and $p_0$ are the rates of the target outcome in the intervention and control arms, they bound causal recall using the observed true-positive rate $\mathrm{TPR}_{ob}$ (the monitor's flag rate on positive cases in the intervention arm) and $R$: $\max\{0,(\mathrm{TPR}_{ob}+R-1)/R\}\le r_c\le\min\{1,\mathrm{TPR}_{ob}/R\}$.
- [Bürger et al. 2026](./#p=burger2026chain) define a score of the same form as Guan et al.'s minimal-criterion rate, $M_0:=\min(1,\pi_Z/R)$ with $R=\max(0,p_{11}-p_{01})/p_{11}$, where $\pi_Z$ is the articulation rate; $M_1$ is the same score restricted to answers for which the CoT is necessary.
- [Anonymous 2026 (implementation)](./#p=anon2026implementation) define the evaluated monitorability $\hat\mu$ as a summary statistic $s$ that compares the monitor's scores $M(\pi)$ with a proxy $\hat P$ for the property: $\hat\mu:=s(M(\pi),\hat P(e(A),t_{\hat P},\pi))$, with the binned mean squared error $\mathrm{BMSE}(X,Y)$ over non-empty bins of $Y$ as the default $s$.
- [Hase & Potts 2026](./#p=hase2026counterfactual) use the unsquared geometric mean, $\sqrt{\mathrm{TPR}\cdot\mathrm{TNR}}$. [Yang et al. 2025](./#p=yang2025investigating) score false negatives, $\Pr(M=0\mid Y_{\text{cue}}=1)$, and false positives, $\Pr(M=1\mid Y_{\text{cue}}=0)$, of the monitor's verdict $M$ against a causal label $Y_{\text{cue}}$.

### 4. Evaluation designs: types of evaluation, default and adversarial conditions, and true labels

84 definitions from 22 papers. Kinds: 34 operational, 20 informal, 20 taxonomies, 5 criteria, 4 formal, 1 failure mode. Stances: 47 proposed, 27 adopted, 10 adapted.

These definitions set out the basic types of monitorability evaluation and the labels the monitor must predict, along with how the monitored behavior is induced and the conditions under which monitoring is tested ([Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring), [Duzan & Cooper Stickland 2026](./#p=duzan2026chainofthought), [OpenAI 2026](./#p=openai2026gpt6astra)). [Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring) distinguish three types, which they call archetypes. Intervention evaluations apply a controlled change to the input or environment and ask whether the monitor can infer the change in reasoning that changed the behavior; process evaluations use problems with a small known set of admissible solution paths; outcome-property evaluations use environments where some property of the output can be labeled unambiguously. Three papers restate this classification: [Anonymous 2026 (CoT reliance)](./#p=anon2026chainofthought), [Duzan & Cooper Stickland 2026](./#p=duzan2026chainofthought) and [OpenAI 2026](./#p=openai2026gpt6astra). [Bürger et al. 2026](./#p=burger2026chain) recast it as influence, process and outcome "monitorability types", while [Wang et al. 2026](./#p=wang2026monitorbench) adapt it into input intervention, outcome justification and solution process, with outcome justification requiring the CoT to "explicitly articulate[] the rationale" for atypical outputs. A second distinction separates default from adversarial conditions: [Emmons et al. 2025 (pragmatic)](./#p=emmons2025pragmatic) measure "default monitorability", [OpenAI 2026](./#p=openai2026gpt6astra) splits non-adversarial from adversarial conditions, [Google DeepMind 2026](./#p=google2026gemini4argon) default from worst-case transparency, and [Duzan & Cooper Stickland 2026](./#p=duzan2026chainofthought) explicit from implicit influence. [Anonymous 2026 (implementation)](./#p=anon2026implementation) separate monitorability from "the procedures we use to estimate it". They note that evaluations score a proxy $\hat P$ of the property, computed from side information hidden from the monitor, and they treat sensitivity "to implementation-level details which we do not expect to change evaluations" as one sign that an evaluation is untrustworthy.

> Intervention evaluations. Inspired by prior work [7, 8, 38], we apply a controlled intervention to the agent’s environment or input, measure whether the agent’s behavior changes due to the intervention, and then ask whether a monitor can infer a specific change in reasoning leading to the change in behavior.
>
> ([Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring), §2)

### 5. Necessity: the task requires the CoT, and the answer depends on it

64 definitions from 29 papers. Kinds: 21 operational, 20 criteria, 9 informal, 9 failure modes, 4 taxonomies, 1 formal. Stances: 43 proposed, 16 adopted, 5 adapted.

These definitions base monitorability on the CoT being needed: if a model cannot do a task without writing out its reasoning, the reasoning has to appear in the trace. [Emmons et al. 2025 (necessity)](./#p=emmons2025chain) define necessity as "the model cannot succeed without it", and [Korbak et al. 2025](./#p=korbak2025chain) separate this necessity from the propensity to think out loud (a tendency to write out reasoning even when the task does not require it). Later definitions make necessity a matter of degree: [Bürger et al. 2026](./#p=burger2026chain) split it into levels 0, 1, 2 and 3.m, [Anonymous 2026 (CoT reliance)](./#p=anon2026chainofthought) measure it as "CoT reliance" (performance with CoT minus performance without it), and [Google DeepMind 2026](./#p=google2026gemini4argon) and [OpenAI 2026](./#p=openai2026gpt6astra) measure what the model can do without CoT (serial depth, the NCRI index, a no-CoT math time horizon). The group also holds the ways necessity fails (reasoning in the latent space instead of the text, filler tokens in place of reasoning, the ability to solve tasks without CoT) and a version for plans in [Fareed 2026](./#p=fareed2026plan): a plan "with no causal effect", detected by seed-invariance (the same outcome in every seeded rollout).

> Necessity: We say CoT is necessary for a task if the model cannot succeed without it. This condition formalizes the requirement that a model must “think out loud.”
>
> ([Emmons et al. 2025 (necessity)](./#p=emmons2025chain), §2)

### 6. Legibility, coverage and checkability: a reader can follow or check the reasoning

108 definitions from 22 papers. Kinds: 36 operational, 27 informal, 14 failure modes, 12 criteria, 11 taxonomies, 8 formal. Stances: 75 proposed, 19 adapted, 14 adopted.

These definitions ask whether the reasoning is accessible to one of four readers. The first is a verifier whose completeness (it accepts correct solutions) and soundness (it cannot be fooled into accepting incorrect ones) are required only in a relaxed form ([Kirchner et al. 2024](./#p=kirchner2024proververifier)). The second is a human with the same language as the person who wrote the prompt, for legibility, and the same expertise, for coverage ([Emmons et al. 2025 (pragmatic)](./#p=emmons2025pragmatic)). The third is a model, assessed by whether it can continue the reasoning successfully ([Jiao et al. 2026](./#p=jiao2026tandem), [Roytburg et al. 2026](./#p=roytburg2026measuring)), by how well it predicts the tokens of the trace ([Jiao et al. 2026](./#p=jiao2026tandem)), or by "final-answer agreement" ([Sullivan & Koller 2026](./#p=sullivan2026language)). The fourth is the monitor itself ([Baker et al. 2025](./#p=baker2025monitoring)). [Roytburg et al. 2026](./#p=roytburg2026measuring) state the disagreement directly: "Prior work defines legibility inconsistently." [Sullivan & Koller 2026](./#p=sullivan2026language) define language drift as novel linguistic behavior that is "non-standard and/or ungrammatical" and prove that "RLVR optimization pressure permits unbounded language drift, while supervised fine-tuning does not" (RLVR is reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards). [MacDermott et al. 2025](./#p=macdermott2025reasoning) deliberately simulate drift by rewarding KL divergence (a measure of how much one probability distribution differs from another), and find no clear qualitative drift while accuracy stays close to its original level. [Jose 2025](./#p=jose2025reasoning) observes "compressed" reasoning and proposes vestigial reasoning as a hypothesis. [Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring) discuss "legibility collapse" by summarizing Jose, while [Schoen et al. 2025](./#p=schoen2025stress) report non-standard terminology that increases during reinforcement learning aimed at capabilities.

> When we find helpful provers and verifiers with such limited completeness and soundness properties, we say that the helpful prover is legible to the verifier. This is analogous to saying that outputs are legible to humans if humans accept them as correct and can’t be fooled by similar-looking incorrect outputs.
>
> ([Kirchner et al. 2024](./#p=kirchner2024proververifier), §1)

### 7. Encoded reasoning and steganography: information in the trace that a reader cannot read

84 definitions from 22 papers. Kinds: 28 operational, 23 failure modes, 15 informal, 9 criteria, 5 taxonomies, 4 formal. Stances: 59 proposed, 19 adopted, 6 adapted.

These definitions concern information that the trace carries and a reader cannot read. They differ on who the reader is: [Lanham et al. 2023](./#p=lanham2023measuring) define encoded reasoning as "not understandable to human readers" and [Schulz 2026](./#p=schulz2026monitor) requires that it be "hidden from humans", while [Skaf et al. 2025](./#p=skaf2025large) define steganography as information "hidden to an external monitor". [Mathew et al. 2024](./#p=mathew2025hidden) and [Zhou & May 2026](./#p=zhou2026conceptual) frame steganography as a game between a colluding sender and receiver on one side and an overseer or a defender who paraphrases the text on the other. The game is measured by how often messages slip past the overseer (bypass rate), how accurately the receiver decodes them (decode accuracy) and how much information the channel can carry (channel capacity). [Roger & Greenblatt 2023](./#p=roger2023preventing) and [Roger 2025](./#p=anthropic2025paraphrases) define paraphrasing tests, which reword the reasoning and check whether performance drops, for whether a model relies on encoded reasoning.

> Encoded reasoning: The benefit from CoT may be attained by LLMs encoding the relevant information in the generated reasoning in a way that is not understandable to human readers (a form of steganography).
>
> ([Lanham et al. 2023](./#p=lanham2023measuring), §1)

### 8. Obfuscation: the trace stops revealing a behavior that persists

### 18. Monitorability and legibility taxes: the costs of keeping models monitorable or legible, of evading monitors and of compressing the CoT

Four papers in the map account for 182 of the 535 citations (one citation is one definition naming one source), and they are borrowed for different things. [Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring) is cited for its evaluation apparatus: 31 of the 74 definitions citing it are in the evaluation-design group and 24 in the metrics group. [Baker et al. 2025](./#p=baker2025monitoring) is cited for concepts and reaches the most papers (39): 17 of the 51 definitions citing it are in the obfuscation group. [Korbak et al. 2025](./#p=korbak2025chain) (29 citing definitions from 27 papers) is cited across the monitors, verbalization and predictive groups, and [Emmons et al. 2025 (necessity)](./#p=emmons2025chain) (28 citing definitions from 17 papers) for necessity (12 definitions).

Citing papers take Baker et al.'s definition in two directions. 7 definitions in the predictive group cite it for the prediction of a property ([Kaufmann et al. 2026](./#p=kaufmann2026aligned): "independent of the model’s “true reasoning process”"). 9 definitions in the verbalization group cite it, 8 of them for a faithfulness requirement ([Occhipinti et al. 2026](./#p=occhipinti2026probing): "Faithfulness is a prerequisite for CoT monitorability"; [Xiong et al. 2026](./#p=xiong2026monitorability): "faithfully reflect the model’s internal decision-making path"), while [Anonymous 2026 (CoT reliance)](./#p=anon2026chainofthought) cites it for the view that monitorability is "an easier objective than faithfulness because of a weaker overall comprehensiveness requirement". The check marks 13 of these 16 restatements supported, 1 adapted and 2 partial, because Baker et al.'s text holds both readings: monitorability is "the ability to monitor for specific properties of agents actions or outputs (monitorability), and not faithfulness in isolation", and "If a monitor $M$ can accurately predict property $v$ from latent $z$, then $z$ must be faithful with respect to $v$ and legible to $M$". The two partial verdicts are the restatements that extend faithfulness from the monitored property to the whole reasoning process: [Xiong et al. 2026](./#p=xiong2026monitorability) and [Reddy et al. 2025](./#p=reddy2025principled) ("the ability to inspect and evaluate a model’s internal reasoning process").

Guan et al.'s definition changes in smaller steps as papers borrow it. [Bürger et al. 2026](./#p=burger2026chain) quote it verbatim; [Anonymous 2026 (improving monitorability)](./#p=anon2026improving) widen the reader to "the monitoring setup"; [Lewis-Lim et al. 2026](./#p=lewislim2026efficient) and [OpenAI 2026](./#p=openai2026gpt6astra) replace "predict" with "recovered" and "infer"; and [Li 2026](./#p=li2026monitorability) adds that "monitorability is fragile under scaling and training pressure", which the check marks partial because Guan et al. report that "RL optimization does not materially decrease monitorability even at the current frontier scale". Four checked definitions rename the evaluation types of [Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring): [Bürger et al. 2026](./#p=burger2026chain) call the archetypes "monitorability types" and intervention "influence", while [Wang et al. 2026](./#p=wang2026monitorbench) call intervention and process "input intervention" and "solution process". A fifth checked definition, the "Grader Hacking Hint" of [Xiong et al. 2026](./#p=xiong2026monitorability), renames Guan et al.'s reward-hacking hint category.

None of the definitions collected from [Korbak et al. 2025](./#p=korbak2025chain) defines monitorability itself. Its 21 definitions define the CoT monitor, the necessity and the propensity to think out loud, the externalized reasoning property, evaluations of and threats to monitorability, and state that "CoT does not need to completely represent the actual reasoning process". Of the 12 definitions in groups 1 and 2 (the two groups that define monitorability) that cite Korbak et al., the check marks 9 supported and 3 adapted. They include the faithfulness wordings of [Xiong et al. 2026](./#p=xiong2026monitorability), [Haskins et al. 2026](./#p=haskins2026training) and [Anonymous 2026 (one draw)](./#p=anon2026one), which match the threat Korbak et al. describe, that optimization "might make this chain of thought less faithfully reflect how models arrive at their decisions".

Emmons et al.'s necessity reaches the citing definitions under its own terms ("CoT uplift", the pass rate with CoT minus the pass rate without it, and "CoT-as-computation", which [Jose et al. 2026](./#p=jose2026strategic) and [Golechha et al. 2026](./#p=golechha2026preliminary) adopt) and under two new names: "CoT reliance" in [Anonymous 2026 (CoT reliance)](./#p=anon2026chainofthought) (renamed, according to the check) and "load-bearing" reasoning in [Duzan & Cooper Stickland 2026](./#p=duzan2026chainofthought). It is also cited for a tool it does not contain: [MacDermott et al. 2025](./#p=macdermott2025reasoning) attribute to it the autorater for legibility and coverage (a language model that rates reasoning traces), and the check finds the autorater in [Emmons et al. 2025 (pragmatic)](./#p=emmons2025pragmatic).

Among sources outside the map, Kutasov et al. 2025 (15 citing definitions from 9 papers) and Greenblatt et al. 2024 (15 from 12 papers) are the most cited, with 6 and 8 of their citing definitions in the scheming group. In the four groups on monitored behaviors (deception, scheming, reward hacking, evaluation awareness), 87 definitions name a source and 10 of them cite a paper in the map, so 77 borrowed definitions of behaviors are not covered by the check.

### Where the definitions disagree

**Does monitorability require faithfulness, require it only for the monitored property, or not require it?**

[Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring) reject the requirement: their notion "does not require a model’s externalized reasoning to faithfully reflect its internal reasoning process". [Baker et al. 2025](./#p=baker2025monitoring) reject causal faithfulness as a requirement ("causal CoTs (see Figure 25) are not a necessary condition for monitorability") but keep faithfulness relative to the monitored property: "If a monitor $M$ can accurately predict property $v$ from latent $z$, then $z$ must be faithful with respect to $v$ and legible to $M$". [Korbak et al. 2025](./#p=korbak2025chain) reject only completeness: "CoT does not need to completely represent the actual reasoning process in order to be a valuable additional safety layer".

On the other side, three papers state the requirement without restricting it to the monitored property: [Jia et al. 2026](./#p=jia2026faithfulness) (CoT is "useful for monitoring language models only when the reasoning trace faithfully reflects the computation that produces the final answer"), [Xiong et al. 2026](./#p=xiong2026monitorability) (traces "should faithfully reflect the model’s internal decision-making path") and [Anonymous 2026 (one draw)](./#p=anon2026one) ("useful for safety only if the reasoning reflects what drives the model’s answers"). Two further papers state related causal requirements. [Bhupatiraju & Nyaupane 2026](./#p=bhupatiraju2026stated) present causal faithfulness as a premise of CoT monitoring and say the mechanism is "only as good as the link" between stated reasoning and the computation that produces the answer. [Shiromani et al. 2026](./#p=shiromani2026necessary) require "provenance" about a particular context feature, and compare monitors that read the text with probes on the model's hidden states, using a label of dependence defined by whether substituting another wrong answer in the hint changes the model’s answer. [Kaufmann et al. 2026](./#p=kaufmann2026aligned) take Baker et al.'s middle position: monitorability is reduced whenever the CoT is non-transparent "with respect to the specific computations the monitor needs to detect". [Bürger et al. 2026](./#p=burger2026chain) tie the requirement to influence rather than to the monitor, holding that a CoT "cannot, and should not, reflect every detail of the model’s computation" but "must articulate the key influential factors and reasoning steps that shaped the answer".

The direct disagreement is between Guan et al. on one side and Jia et al., Xiong et al., Anonymous 2026 (one draw), Bhupatiraju & Nyaupane and Shiromani et al. on the other. Korbak et al. reject a complete representation, not faithfulness, and the check marks Korbak et al. supported for the faithfulness wordings of [Xiong et al. 2026](./#p=xiong2026monitorability), [Haskins et al. 2026](./#p=haskins2026training) and [Anonymous 2026 (one draw)](./#p=anon2026one).

**Is monitorability a property of the model alone, or of the model and the monitor?**

[Xiong et al. 2026](./#p=xiong2026monitorability) write that "”monitorability” refers to the intrinsic properties of the evaluated model, independent of the monitor’s specific capabilities", and [Wang et al. 2026](./#p=wang2026monitorbench) give the monitor "exact decision-critical factors to look for", which they say "ensures monitorability reflects the properties of the evaluated LLM and is independent of the monitor’s specific capabilities". [Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring), whose minimal-criterion score both papers use, call monitorability "a two-sided property" that depends on "the capacity of the monitor to extract useful information", and [Algaba et al. 2026](./#p=algaba2026reasoning), [Anonymous 2026 (CoT reliance)](./#p=anon2026chainofthought), [Anonymous 2026 (improving monitorability)](./#p=anon2026improving) and [Emmons et al. 2025 (pragmatic)](./#p=emmons2025pragmatic) make the monitor part of the definition. Xiong et al.'s own score cuts against their statement: the same section defines g-mean² "with a higher score indicating better monitorability with respect to the applied monitor".

**Does necessity make a CoT monitorable?**

[Emmons et al. 2025 (necessity)](./#p=emmons2025chain) enforce necessity by making the bad behavior harder and state that "this forces the model to expose its reasoning, making it monitorable", while finding that models "can learn to obscure their intentions, but only when given significant help". [Schulz 2026](./#p=schulz2026monitor) argues that CoT necessity forces a model to "externalize its reasoning in some form", but warns that encoded or steganographic reasoning may remain uninterpretable to a monitor; [Turpin et al. 2025](./#p=turpin2025teaching) argue that, for complex tasks requiring CoT, "a lack of verbalization would indicate the model is not reward hacking".

[Korbak et al. 2025](./#p=korbak2025chain) hold that "The externalized reasoning property does not guarantee monitorability", and [Anonymous 2026 (CoT reliance)](./#p=anon2026chainofthought) find that "monitorability errors can arise in other ways besides non-verbalization, including burying the verbalization within a long CoT that is difficult for the monitor to follow". [Anwar et al. 2026 (information theory)](./#p=anwar2026analyzing) give the information-theoretic version of the gap: the necessary condition $I(O;Z\mid X)>0$ (the CoT carries some information about the output beyond the prompt) "is implied by CoT being necessary for solving the task" but "can also be fulfilled in other cases, such as when CoT is a post-hoc explanation of the model output".

**Does leaving the evidence out of the CoT count as obfuscation?**

[Wang et al. 2026](./#p=wang2026monitorbench) distinguish omission from obfuscation. For factors that affect only final answers, they inspected by hand the categories with a large drop in monitorability under stress tests, including Cue (Answer) and Goal (Deception), and found a consistent pattern across all inspected cases in both stress-test settings: the model "largely omits explicit references" to those factors, rather than using the paraphrasing, language switching or encoding they had hypothesized. [Schulz 2026](./#p=schulz2026monitor) calls omission obfuscation (models that "still reward hack while not reasoning about it in their CoT" show "obfuscated reasoning"), and so do [Anwar et al. 2026 (information theory)](./#p=anwar2026analyzing) ("obfuscation via suppression: they learn to omit critical reasoning steps"). [Haskins et al. 2026](./#p=haskins2026training) measure successful obfuscation by the fraction of all responses that both perform the target deceptive behavior and escape the monitor. An undetected rate above both the behavior-only control and the no-reasoning baseline indicates that "the model is actively using its CoT to perform the behavior while evading the monitor".

**Is obfuscation intentional?**

[Mao et al. 2026](./#p=mao2026taming) define CoT obfuscation as "an operational, non-intentional training outcome" and cite [Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring), [Drori et al. 2025](./#p=drori2025output) and [Haskins et al. 2026](./#p=haskins2026training) for it. The check marks the Haskins citation partial: Haskins et al. report that "models with both the means (CoT control) and motive (monitor knowledge) can obfuscate their reasoning, even without optimization pressure". [Korbak et al. 2025](./#p=korbak2025chain) write that "Future models may become able to avoid detection by overriding their propensity to think out loud, and, when reasoning is required, to deliberately obfuscate it", and [Jose et al. 2026](./#p=jose2026strategic) find that "intrinsic motivation to obfuscate leads to far better performance at evading a monitor than following instructions".

**Is encoded reasoning hidden from humans or from the monitor?**

[Lanham et al. 2023](./#p=lanham2023measuring) define encoded reasoning as "not understandable to human readers", and [Schulz 2026](./#p=schulz2026monitor) excludes from it reasoning that fools an LLM monitor but stays readable: "This does not fit the definition of encoded reasoning, as the reasoning is not hidden from humans". [Skaf et al. 2025](./#p=skaf2025large) define steganography as information "hidden to an external monitor" and require that "The monitor cannot decode the encodings, nor detect their presence", and [Anwar et al. 2026 (information theory)](./#p=anwar2026analyzing) call a CoT whose mistakes "the model knows to ignore ... but the monitor does not" "a weak form of steganography". Under the definition of [Skaf et al. 2025](./#p=skaf2025large), an encoding of harmful planning in load-bearing tokens (tokens that carry information the model uses) can be steganographic even if humans can read it, provided the monitor can neither decode it nor detect its presence; encodings that the monitor can detect but not decode are "Overtly encoded CoT". Under the definition of [Schulz 2026](./#p=schulz2026monitor), reasoning visible to humans is not encoded reasoning.

**Legible to whom: a verifier, a human or the monitor?**

[Kirchner et al. 2024](./#p=kirchner2024proververifier) make legibility checkability by a verifier, with completeness and soundness both required: outputs are legible "if humans accept them as correct and can’t be fooled by similar-looking incorrect outputs". [Emmons et al. 2025 (pragmatic)](./#p=emmons2025pragmatic) make it readability by "a human with the same language capabilities as the user asking the model", so that switching languages "results in a Legibility score of 0", while [Baker et al. 2025](./#p=baker2025monitoring) make it relative to the monitor: "if an agent’s CoT tokens are in French but the monitor only understands English, the monitor will fail due to illegibility", and "a different bilingual monitor would find it plausible". [Jiao et al. 2026](./#p=jiao2026tandem) replace checking by continuation ("whether a designated weaker generator can take over the senior’s partial trajectory and still complete the task successfully"), and [Roytburg et al. 2026](./#p=roytburg2026measuring) write that "Prior work defines legibility inconsistently". The check marks Roytburg et al.'s restatement of Kirchner et al. partial, because Kirchner et al. call a prover legible only when completeness also holds.

**Deception, covert action, scheming, reward hacking and obfuscation: where are the boundaries?**

[Schoen et al. 2025](./#p=schoen2025stress) define covert actions so that "the definition of covert actions does not rely on intent on the AI system’s part", "in contrast to the related concept of deception (Ward et al., 2023)", while [Bu et al. 2026](./#p=bu2026spadebench) define deception by its effect, "behavior that induces false beliefs in humans or other intelligent systems", citing the same Ward et al. (2023). The characterizations of scheming differ: [Schoen et al. 2025](./#p=schoen2025stress) define it as "the covert pursuit of misaligned goals while hiding true capabilities and objectives"; [Google DeepMind 2026](./#p=google2026gemini4argon) specify a model that "knowingly and covertly pursues objectives misaligned with its developer's intention"; [Betley et al. 2025](./#p=betley2025tell) associate it with "strategically deceiving humans in order to further their goals"; [Emmons et al. 2025 (necessity)](./#p=emmons2025chain) describe its risk as models that "might strategically subvert human control"; and [Bu et al. 2026](./#p=bu2026spadebench) say it "typically involves" long-horizon strategic goal pursuit, persistent hidden objectives or coordinated planning across multiple episodes. Betley et al.'s wording puts deceiving humans at the core, Google DeepMind's makes knowledge a condition, and Schoen et al. require covertness but not intent for the actions they measure; [Li et al. 2025](./#p=li2026spilling) treat the same notion as a capability, "the model’s ability to covertly pursue its own objective".

Reward hacking sits on both sides of the line around deception. [Taufeeque et al. 2026](./#p=taufeeque2026obfuscation) list it among "Deceptive behaviors" and call reward-hacking policies that the probe catches "Blatant deception", and [Anonymous 2026 (honesty adapters)](./#p=anon2026traininghonesty) use School of Reward Hacks, where the assistant "either games an explicitly stated, gameable evaluation criterion or answers honestly", as a deception benchmark. [Baker et al. 2025](./#p=baker2025monitoring) and [Farquhar et al. 2025](./#p=farquhar2025mona) define reward hacking without reference to deception or induced false beliefs ("Exploiting flaws or misspecifications in learning objectives"; "achieves a high reward in a way the system designer would not want if they understood"), and Schoen et al. file it among the behavioral tags that "describe the type of scheming behavior being tested".

Obfuscation joins the three in the same experiments. In Taufeeque et al., a model trained against a deception detector "either remains honest, or becomes deceptive via two possible obfuscation strategies". In [Haskins et al. 2026](./#p=haskins2026training), the behavior hidden from the monitor is deception about known product defects, and "detection is only counted over deceptive responses". In [Jose et al. 2026](./#p=jose2026strategic), it is reasoning that fakes alignment. And [Lee et al. 2026](./#p=lee2026training) train self-incrimination to make "covert misbehavior observable".

**Can monitorability be scored without a monitor?**

[Meek et al. 2025](./#p=meek2025measuring) score monitorability as "the average between the faithfulness and verbosity metrics", a score built from verbalization rates rather than from a monitor's predictions, and [Occhipinti et al. 2026](./#p=occhipinti2026probing) count as monitorable "all traces where the hint’s influence is visible". [Yang et al. 2025](./#p=yang2025investigating) report "a gap between being monitorable and being monitored correctly": "Even when CoT is always faithful, the monitor itself may fail to strike the right balance." [Young 2026](./#p=young2026why) restates Meek et al. as showing reasoning "monitorable (detectable by a judge) without being faithful"; the check marks this partial, because Meek et al. show the reverse divergence: "models can appear faithful yet remain hard to monitor when they leave out key factors".

**What does the minimal-criterion score measure?**

[Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring) define minimal-criterion sensitivity (the minimal-criterion true-positive rate of group 3) from the requirement that "the monitor should trigger at least as often as this rate in the intervention arm"; seven other papers reuse or adapt this form, with six retaining a monitor flag rate and [Bürger et al. 2026](./#p=burger2026chain) using the CoT's articulation rate. [Anonymous 2026 (improving monitorability)](./#p=anon2026improving) prove, under Assumption 1 for stable binary intervention experiments with a positive average effect, that minimal-criterion sensitivity is the upper endpoint of the bounds on causal recall and "does not generally identify it"; when the control-arm target-outcome rate is zero, both bounds equal observed recall. Because control-arm specificity (true-negative rate) varies little across checkpoints in their evaluations, they replace the geometric mean of the two specificities in the composite score of [Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring) with intervention-arm specificity alone ($\text{g-mean}^2_{\text{int}}:=\mathrm{TPR}_{\mathrm{mc}}\cdot\mathrm{TNR}_{X=1}$). [Duzan & Cooper Stickland 2026](./#p=duzan2026chainofthought) keep both specificities but multiply them with the plain detection rate on behavioral-shift cases, and [Xiong et al. 2026](./#p=xiong2026monitorability) warn of a "false sense of monitorability": "a model might achieve a high $\text{g-mean}^{2}$ score simply by overfitting to the prompt’s constraints".
