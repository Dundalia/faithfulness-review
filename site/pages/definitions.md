# Definitions of Faithfulness, Counterfactual and Monitorability

This page collects how the papers in the map define three terms: *faithfulness*, *counterfactual* and *monitorability*. It rests on 1,197 definitions quoted from 201 of the 217 entries. The other 16 entries use none of the terms in a defining way. Each definition is quoted verbatim with its location in the paper, its kind (informal, formal, operational, criterion, failure mode or taxonomy) and its stance: proposed by the paper, or adopted or adapted from earlier work, with the source. The full set is [`definitions.csv`](https://github.com/Dundalia/faithfulness-review/blob/main/definitions.csv), one row per definition, and the Definitions tab of the dashboard lets you filter it.

For each term, the definitions are grouped into clusters of meaning. Each section then traces the lineage of the borrowed definitions, checks a sample of attributions against the cited sources, and lists the points on which the clusters disagree. In the tables, text in quotation marks is copied from the paper and text without quotation marks is a gloss.

## Main findings

**Faithfulness has one definition in words and six in practice.** Fifty entries borrow from [Jacovi & Goldberg 2020](./#p=jacovi2020towards), for whom a faithful interpretation "accurately represents the reasoning process behind the model’s prediction". The tests built under that phrase measure different properties: whether the trace discloses the factors that changed the answer, whether the answer causally depends on the trace, whether trace and answer agree, whether the explanation lets an observer predict the model, whether it marks the inputs that matter, and whether it matches the model's internal computation. These verdicts can conflict. An answer that survives a corrupted chain of thought is unfaithful under the causal-dependence tests ([Lanham et al. 2023](./#p=lanham2023measuring)) and good behavior for [Fareed 2026](./#p=fareed2026plan). An unverbalized cue that changed the answer is the central case of unfaithfulness for [Chen et al. 2025](./#p=chen2025reasoning) and an incomplete account, not an unfaithful one, for [Zaman & Srivastava 2025](./#p=zaman2025chainofthought).

**A counterfactual is almost always a paired run.** The model is run on an original and on a variant that differs from it in one specified respect, and the two outputs are compared. The definitions differ in what is varied (the input, the model's own reasoning or an internal state), in whether the variant must change the output to count, and in what the comparison is used to judge (an explanation, the model's reliance on a feature, or a training target).

**Monitorability is defined relative to a monitor and often cited as a faithfulness requirement.** Most definitions make it a relation between a model and a reader: whether a given monitor can predict a specified property of the model's behavior from what the model exposes ([Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring)). A second group makes it a property of the trace alone, its faithful and complete verbalization of what drove the answer, and cites for this the sources that reject it. [Korbak et al. 2025](./#p=korbak2025chain), borrowed by 19 entries, never defines monitorability and writes that the chain of thought "does not need to completely represent the actual reasoning process". [Baker et al. 2025](./#p=baker2025monitoring), borrowed by 26, requires faithfulness only with respect to the property being monitored.

**The three terms meet in the same experiments.** Cue insertion and edits to the reasoning trace serve as counterfactuals in one paper, faithfulness tests in another and monitorability evaluations in a third. Some papers merge two of the terms. [Little 2026](./#p=little2026length) calls a chain faithful "when an LLM monitor can tell from it that the hint influenced the answer", [Hase & Potts 2026](./#p=hase2026counterfactual) measure faithfulness with a reasoning simulator that is "interchangeable with reasoning monitor", and [Korbak et al. 2025](./#p=korbak2025chain) treat faithfulness evaluations as a proxy measure for monitorability. [Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring) state that their notion of monitorability "does not require a model’s externalized reasoning to faithfully reflect its internal reasoning process".

**Borrowed definitions are often misattributed.** The synthesis checked 31 attributions against the cited sources, choosing the suspected mismatches that matter most. 22 were confirmed, 5 were partly supported and 4 could not be settled. Examples: "parametric faithfulness" credited to [Chen et al. 2025](./#p=chen2025reasoning), which never uses the term (it is from [Tutek et al. 2025](./#p=tutek2025measuring)); counterfactual simulatability credited to [Hase et al. 2020](./#p=hase2020leakageadjusted), whose simulator only predicts the output on the explained input; a hint setting credited to [Lanham et al. 2023](./#p=lanham2023measuring), which has no hint experiments. The checks targeted suspected mismatches, so these counts are not an error rate for the literature.

## How this page was made

1. **Excerpts.** `scripts/definitions.py excerpt` keeps each section of a paper's cached full text that mentions faithful\*, counterfactual\*, monitorab\*, legib\*, obfuscat\*, steganograph\* or "encoded reasoning", without the reference list and most appendices. A pilot on nine papers showed that keeping only the paragraphs that use a term loses definitions written as list items or named properties, so whole sections are kept.
2. **Extraction.** Claude agents, each with about twelve papers, recorded every passage that says what one of the terms or a companion term means. Each agent checked its quotes with `scripts/definitions.py check`. All 1,197 quotes are found verbatim in the full texts, up to whitespace and curly quotes.
3. **Synthesis.** One agent per term grouped the definitions into clusters (every definition belongs to exactly one, recorded in the `cluster` column), traced the lineage, checked up to ten attributions against the source texts, and wrote the section below. The raw outputs are in [`runs/D_definitions/`](https://github.com/Dundalia/faithfulness-review/tree/main/runs/D_definitions).

The extraction and the clusters are machine-made. Check a quote in the paper before citing it, and read the clusters as one grouping of the data rather than a taxonomy the papers share. Sources outside the map were not checked, including Atanasova et al. (2023), which twelve entries borrow for the counterfactual test of explanations.

## Faithfulness

The extraction holds 740 definitions in the faithfulness family, from 168 of the 217 papers. 421 define faithfulness or unfaithfulness itself; 319 define a companion term that the paper ties to it, such as plausibility, simulatability, self-consistency, post-hoc rationalization or verbalization. By kind there are 210 informal definitions, 182 operational ones, 123 criteria, 78 failure modes, 75 formal definitions and 72 taxonomies; by stance 402 are proposed, 261 adopted and 77 adapted. In one sentence: most papers name [Jacovi & Goldberg 2020](./#p=jacovi2020towards)'s "accurately represents the reasoning process behind the model’s prediction" as what faithfulness means, but the tests built under that phrase measure six different properties (disclosure of influential factors, causal dependence of the answer on the reasoning, agreement between reasoning and answer, predictive value to an observer, marking of important inputs, and correspondence with internal computation), and these can give opposite verdicts on the same trace.

### Accurate representation of the reasoning process

This is the umbrella answer: an explanation or CoT is faithful when it accurately represents, reflects or describes the process by which the model reached its output. Most members state the target without saying how to test it (79 of the 126 are informal, 83 adopted). The cluster also holds the 30 taxonomies that split the umbrella into kinds of test (contextual vs parametric, behavioural vs mechanistic, verbalization vs intervention, interpretability vs NLG faithfulness); they classify ways of operationalizing the umbrella rather than proposing a new property, so they are kept here.

>
> ([Jacovi & Goldberg 2020](./#p=jacovi2020towards), §1)

The only formal member is a type signature: [Zaman & Srivastava 2025](./#p=zaman2025causal) define a faithfulness metric as a real-valued function $\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},M)\in\mathbb{R}$ of an input, output, explanation and model, and evaluate candidate metrics by *diagnosticity*, how often a metric prefers a faithful over an unfaithful explanation, with the two built by knowledge editing so that each explanation is unfaithful to the model that produced the other.

### Disclosure of the factors that drove the answer

Here a trace is faithful when it states the factors that actually moved the answer. The standard test inserts a cue (a suggested answer, a metadata hint, reordered options), keeps the cases where the answer moves to the cued option, and asks whether the CoT mentions the cue and, in the stricter versions, whether it presents the cue as a reason. Variants extend the test from planted cues to concepts ([Matton et al. 2025](./#p=matton2025walk); [Arcuschin et al. 2026](./#p=arcuschin2026biases)), to self-reports of which factors mattered ([Zhang et al. 2026](./#p=zhang2026audit)), and to pairs of logically opposite questions answered with unverbalized bias ([Arcuschin et al. 2025](./#p=arcuschin2025chain)).

> In order to be faithful in this setup, models must either acknowledge any biases affecting their predictions or give predictions unaffected by bias.
>
> ([Turpin et al. 2023](./#p=turpin2023language), §2)

- [Chen et al. 2025](./#p=chen2025reasoning), over pairs of an unhinted prompt $x_u$ and a hinted prompt $x_h$ with answers $a_u,a_h$ and hinted CoT $c_h$:
  $$\mathrm{Faith}(M)=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{1}[c_h\text{ verbalizes the hint }h]\;\big|\;a_u\neq h,\ a_h=h\big],$$
  normalized by $\alpha=1-q/((n-2)p)$ to discount answer changes that random switching would produce, and clipped at 1.
- [Matton et al. 2025](./#p=matton2025walk), causal concept faithfulness on a question $\mathbf{x}$ with concepts $\mathcal{C}$: $\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{PCC}\big(\mathrm{CE}(\mathbf{x},\mathcal{C}),\,\mathrm{EE}(\mathbf{x},\mathcal{C})\big)$, the Pearson correlation between each concept's causal effect on the answer and its explanation-implied effect; the dataset score is the mean of $\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x})$.
- [Yang et al. 2025](./#p=yang2025investigating): with $Y_{\mathrm{cue}}=1$ when the answer flips to the cued option and $V=1$ when the cue is verbalized, unfaithfulness is the discrepancy between the two, and the case of interest is $Y_{\mathrm{cue}}=1,\ V=0$.
- [Arcuschin et al. 2026](./#p=arcuschin2026biases): a concept $c$ is an unverbalized bias if (1) over discordant pairs of $+/-$ variants of the concept $|D^{+}|\neq|D^{-}|$ by a McNemar test, and (2) the verbalization rate on those pairs is at most $\tau$ ($\tau=0.3$).

### Causal dependence of the answer on the stated reasoning

Here a trace is faithful to the extent that the answer depends on it: truncating it, inserting a mistake, paraphrasing it, replacing it with filler, unlearning a step from the weights or patching in a chain from another problem should change the answer. Post-hoc reasoning, produced after the answer is already fixed, is the central failure, and this cluster holds most of the failure-mode entries (37 of 183: post-hoc rationalization, reasoning theater, performative or decorative CoT, shortcuts that bypass the chain). [Lanham et al. 2023](./#p=lanham2023measuring)'s tests are the template; later work recasts them in mediation terms (the instruction should act on the answer only through the CoT) or moves the intervention into the parameters ([Tutek et al. 2025](./#p=tutek2025measuring)'s parametric faithfulness).

> A reasoning trace is faithful if the model’s answer causally depends on the intermediate reasoning steps.
>
> ([Swaroop et al. 2025](./#p=swaroop2025lift), §2)

### Agreement between the stated reasoning and the answer or action

Here faithfulness is a relation read off the output: the answer, or for agents the action, follows from, is entailed by, or agrees with what the reasoning says. ### Input importance: the explanation marks the inputs the prediction depends on

This is the feature-attribution answer carried over from pre-LLM interpretability: an explanation is faithful if the input parts it marks as important are the ones whose removal or alteration changes the prediction (erasure, comprehensiveness, sufficiency), or if the inputs that drive the prediction also drive the explanation (attribution agreement, CC-SHAP). Self-explanation work applies the same logic by editing the input according to the model's own attribution, redaction or counterfactual explanation and checking whether the prediction flips ([Madsen et al. 2024](./#p=madsen2024self); [Doi et al. 2025](./#p=doi2025investigating)). ### Correspondence with internal computation

Here faithfulness is checked against the model's internals rather than its behaviour: a CoT step is faithful if it describes a process that took place in the model, as shown by probes, attribution graphs, circuits or concept-level ablations. Several members present this as the full notion that behavioural tests only approximate ([Jia & Mu 2026](./#p=jia2026decorative); [Anonymous 2026, *GRACE*](./#p=anon2026grace)), and the introspection papers use the same idea under the name grounding: a self-report must share a causal mechanism with the behaviour it reports, not only match it ([Atkinson et al. 2026](./#p=atkinson2026identifying)). One member applies the word to a different object: [Lindsey et al. 2025](./#p=lindsey2025biology) ask whether a transcoder replacement is a causally faithful model of the MLP it replaces.

**Jacovi & Goldberg's sentence and its paraphrases.** 49 papers cite Jacovi & Goldberg for what faithfulness is (53 entries), and 13 more entries cite them for plausibility. Jacovi & Goldberg word it two ways, "accurately represents the reasoning process behind the model’s prediction" and "how accurately it reflects the true reasoning process of the model". 19 of the 53 entries keep "reasoning process", and only 8 keep "accurately represent". The others substitute "decision process", "decision-making process", internal computation, behaviour, factors or a causal verb. These substitutions move the target in four directions, and each direction is one of the clusters above:

- toward internal computation: "the reasoning accurately describe the models internal computation" ([Lewis-Lim et al. 2026](./#p=lewislim2026efficient)), "the degree to which its reasoning traces reflect its internal decision-making" ([Cheah et al. 2026](./#p=cheah2026training));
- toward behaviour: "explanations should reflect how the model actually behaves, not just sound plausible" ([Malladi & Srivastava 2026](./#p=malladi2026directly)), "whether explanations support actual model behavior" ([Hase et al. 2020](./#p=hase2020leakageadjusted));
- toward causal role: "the degree to which a model’s stated reasoning genuinely causes its final prediction, rather than merely accompanying it" ([Somov et al. 2026](./#p=somov2026breaking)), "the degree to which it accurately represents or contributes to the underlying reasoning of the model" ([Wang et al. 2026](./#p=wang2026concept));
- toward factors: "An explanation is faithful if it reflects the factors behind a model’s prediction" ([Anonymous 2026, *One Draw Is Not Enough*](./#p=anon2026one)).

[Chen et al. 2025](./#p=chen2025reasoning) adapt the sentence in two ways at once. They relax it to "the key decision factors and reasoning chains" and add a predictive clause, that these should "enable us to predict how it would process similar inputs during deployment". Their operational test then uses only the first part, and 34 disclosure entries borrow from it.

**Plausibility drifts from persuasiveness to correctness.** For Jacovi & Goldberg plausibility is "how convincing the interpretation is to humans", and [Lyu et al. 2023](./#p=lyu2023faithful) keep that sense ("a.k.a. persuasiveness or understandability"). [Chen et al. 2023](./#p=pmlr-v235-chen24bl) write instead "Plausibility evaluates humans’ preference of an explanation based on its factual correctness and logical coherence", citing Herman 2017, Lage et al. 2019 and Jacovi & Goldberg. The same sentence recurs in [Chen et al. 2024](./#p=chen2025consistent), and [Chen et al. 2025](./#p=chen2025reasoning) have "the factual correctness of a CoT and how convincingly it justifies the model’s response". [Cahlik et al. 2025](./#p=cahlik2025reasoninggrounded) reduce plausibility to whether "the explanations are logically sound", and [Lyu et al. 2023](./#p=lyu2023faithful) operationalize it by asking "how often is the reasoning chain truly correct". Once plausibility means correctness, it can no longer be told apart from the validity-based definitions in the agreement cluster, such as [Xu et al. 2024](./#p=xu2024faithful)'s "’faithful’ if both the answer and the process are correct and logical".

**Lanham et al.'s tests become per-trace verdicts.** [Lanham et al. 2023](./#p=lanham2023measuring) report early answering and adding mistakes as task-level measures of post-hoc reasoning, read them with a hedge ("the model is likely not ignoring the CoT") and state that "Reasoning not being post-hoc does not guarantee faithfulness, nor does being post-hoc exclude faithfulness." Borrowers turn the tests into labels on single traces. For early answering, [Tanneru et al. 2024](./#p=tanneru2024hardness) write that it "considers a generated CoT to be faithful if truncating that CoT causes the model to change its final response"; for adding mistakes, [Tutek et al. 2025](./#p=tutek2025measuring) write "If the prediction of the model changes, the CoT is considered faithful". The filler-tokens test changes its question. Lanham et al. ask whether filler tokens alone account for CoT's accuracy gain ("If the filler tokens provide a significant performance improvement, then the CoT may be unfaithful"); [Zaman & Srivastava 2025](./#p=zaman2025chainofthought) instead replace the CoT with ellipses and call it "unfaithful if this corruption does not change the original prediction". One adoption reverses Lanham et al.'s caveat (see the attribution check).

**Cue tests: from counterfactual simulatability to verbalization rates.** [Turpin et al. 2023](./#p=turpin2023language) framed unfaithfulness predictively: reasoning that is plausible on one example can be "misleading about how the system will make predictions on other examples (in which case we also call it unfaithful)". They measured it by accuracy drops and by prediction pairs that change when the biasing feature is added. [Chen et al. 2025](./#p=chen2025reasoning) made verbalization itself the score and required both mention and dependence, and [Chua & Evans 2025](./#p=chua2025deepseek) required the CoT to "describe the switching effect of the cue". Downstream the requirement splits. [Meek et al. 2025](./#p=meek2025measuring) score "the presence of the given cue in a model’s reasoning trace", and [Nguyen et al. 2026](./#p=nguyen2026generalization), "Following Meek et al. (2025)", "label a CoT trace as faithful when it mentions the injected cue". [Little 2026](./#p=little2026length) asks only whether "an LLM monitor can tell from it that the hint influenced the answer". [Anonymous 2026, *One Draw Is Not Enough*](./#p=anon2026one), [egan 2026](./#p=egan2026hint) and [Kudo et al. 2024](./#p=kudo2024llms) keep both conditions. [Young 2026](./#p=young2026measuring) concludes that the resulting numbers form "a family of related constructs whose values depend critically on the measurement instrument". The predictive framing survives in [Occhipinti et al. 2026](./#p=occhipinti2026probing) ("based on the counterfactual simulatability of CoT reasoning (Turpin et al., 2023)") and in [Turpin et al. 2025](./#p=turpin2025teaching), whose statement that the cue tests "operationalize the counterfactual simulatability notion of faithfulness" is catalogued in the [Counterfactual](#counterfactual) section.

**Simulatability becomes a faithfulness score.** Forward simulation (Doshi-Velez & Kim 2017, outside the map) leads to [Hase et al. 2020](./#p=hase2020leakageadjusted)'s leakage-adjusted simulatability. [Wang et al. 2023](./#p=wang2023scott), [Wang et al. 2022](./#p=wang2023pinto), [Paul et al. 2024](./#p=paul2024making) and [Chen et al. 2022](./#p=chen2022rev) then use LAS directly as the faithfulness of rationales ("To evaluate the faithfulness of the rationales generated by the student, we use LAS"). [Chen et al. 2023](./#p=pmlr-v235-chen24bl) place simulatability under faithfulness, "a special case of faithfulness, which requires the output predictor to be humans", and are followed by [Hong & Roth 2026](./#p=hong2026llm); [Chen et al. 2025](./#p=chen2025reasoning) call it "a stricter form of faithfulness". **Self-consistency and "parametric faithfulness".** [Parcalabescu & Frank 2023](./#p=parcalabescu2023measuring) argue that input- and CoT-perturbation tests measure self-consistency, "a necessary, but not sufficient test for faithfulness". ### Attribution check

### Tensions

1. **Is an answer that survives a corrupted chain a sign of unfaithfulness?** Under the causal-dependence tests, yes. Adding mistakes counts an unchanged answer against the trace ([Lanham et al. 2023](./#p=lanham2023measuring); [Tutek et al. 2025](./#p=tutek2025measuring); [Zaman & Srivastava 2025](./#p=zaman2025causal)), and [Akter et al. 2025](./#p=akter2025causal) require that "A faithful model, upon processing the logically inconsistent trace, should change its prediction or at least become less certain." [Cox et al. 2026](./#p=cox2026posthoc) accept the verdict while noting its cost: answering correctly despite a flawed scratchpad "is perhaps the preferred behavior, but it would constitute unfaithful reasoning". [Fareed 2026](./#p=fareed2026plan) rejects it: "corruption cannot distinguish unfaithfulness from justified correction, since ignoring a broken chain is good behavior". [Jia & Mu 2026](./#p=jia2026decorative) separate "silent bypass" from "self-correction", which a single answer-change rate merges.

2. **Is an unverbalized but influential cue unfaithfulness or incompleteness?** For the disclosure cluster it is the central case: "a CoT that does not verbalize the hint while using the hint is clearly unfaithful" ([Chen et al. 2025](./#p=chen2025reasoning); likewise [Turpin et al. 2023](./#p=turpin2023language), [Chua & Evans 2025](./#p=chua2025deepseek), [Arcuschin et al. 2026](./#p=arcuschin2026biases)). [Zaman & Srivastava 2025](./#p=zaman2025chainofthought) answer that the test "confuses unfaithfulness with incompleteness, the lossy compression needed to turn distributed transformer computation into a linear natural language narrative", and that it measures "verbalized sensitivity", which "is not the same as faithfulness". [Anonymous 2026, *One Draw Is Not Enough*](./#p=anon2026one) ("A trace that omits the cue may be incomplete rather than unfaithful") and [Nguyen et al. 2026](./#p=nguyen2026generalization) ("cue acknowledgment does not establish faithfulness in the conventional sense") take the same side.

3. **Which cue cases count, and is mention enough?** An unchanged answer is faithful for [Turpin et al. 2023](./#p=turpin2023language), whose models may "give predictions unaffected by bias", and for [Turpin et al. 2025](./#p=turpin2025teaching): "If the model’s answer does not change between the prompts, we say it is faithful." [Chua & Evans 2025](./#p=chua2025deepseek) and [Chen et al. 2025](./#p=chen2025reasoning) score only switches ("We only test for faithfulness on the switched examples"). [Meek et al. 2025](./#p=meek2025measuring) score non-switches too, and a model that does not discuss the cue counts as "not being faithful" even when its answer did not move. Mention alone suffices for [Meek et al. 2025](./#p=meek2025measuring) and [Nguyen et al. 2026](./#p=nguyen2026generalization). [Chen et al. 2025](./#p=chen2025reasoning), [egan 2026](./#p=egan2026hint) and [Anonymous 2026, *One Draw Is Not Enough*](./#p=anon2026one) also require that the CoT depend on the cue, and [Young 2026](./#p=young2026measuring) shows that the two classifiers measure different constructs ("mention" vs "epistemic dependence").

4. **Is agreement between reasoning and answer faithfulness?** The agreement cluster says yes: "we use the term faithfulness to mean whether the model’s final answer is consistent with its generated chain-of-thought reasoning" ([Zhao et al. 2026](./#p=zhao2026robustness); also [Li et al. 2024](./#p=li2025better), [Cahlik et al. 2025](./#p=cahlik2025reasoninggrounded)). Systems whose answer is computed from the chain claim faithfulness by construction ([Lyu et al. 2023](./#p=lyu2023faithful); [Pan et al. 2023](./#p=pan2023logiclm); [Tafjord et al. 2022](./#p=tafjord2022entailer)). Others rule this out. [Turpin et al. 2023](./#p=turpin2023language) call reasoning that is "coherent and consistent with its prediction" merely plausible, and possibly unfaithful. [Somov et al. 2026](./#p=somov2026breaking) write that "self-consistency alone does not establish causal reliance", and [Emmons et al. 2025](./#p=emmons2025pragmatic) list "thought-response consistency" as distinct from faithfulness. [Lyu et al. 2023](./#p=lyu2023faithful) concede the point for unconstrained chains: "there is no known inconsistency between the reasoning chain and the answer, but this still does not imply that the reasoning chain is a faithful explanation". [Zhao et al. 2026](./#p=zhao2026robustness) themselves call their measure weaker than "the stronger notion of explanation faithfulness in the interpretability literature".

5. **Behavioural tests or internals?** The causal-dependence, prediction and disclosure clusters judge faithfulness from inputs and outputs, and [Arcuschin et al. 2025](./#p=arcuschin2025chain) ("behavioural faithfulness") and [Malladi & Srivastava 2026](./#p=malladi2026directly) ("behavioral faithfulness") name their notions after this. The mechanism cluster and the self-consistency critics deny that such tests can settle the question. For [Parcalabescu & Frank 2023](./#p=parcalabescu2023measuring) and [Baker et al. 2025](./#p=baker2025monitoring) any such test "actually measures self-consistency rather than faithfulness". [Anonymous 2026, *GRACE*](./#p=anon2026grace) write that they "cannot separate a model whose answer is supported by a different mechanism than its CoT suggests from one whose answer is simply robust to perturbation". [Jia & Mu 2026](./#p=jia2026decorative) call load-bearingness "distinct from full mechanistic faithfulness". [Atkinson et al. 2026](./#p=atkinson2026identifying) make the split explicit for self-reports: a "correct confabulator" is faithful in the behavioural sense but not grounded. [Macar et al. 2025](./#p=macar2025thought) add the converse case: "CoT text can causally impact behavior even if it does not straightforwardly “explain” model internals."

6. **May correctness enter the definition?** Several agreement-cluster definitions tie faithfulness to correct reasoning or answers. [Xu et al. 2024](./#p=xu2024faithful): "’unfaithful’ if the answer is correct but the process is not". [Paul et al. 2024](./#p=paul2024making): "how reliably the model uses its reasoning steps to arrive at a correct answer". [Wang et al. 2025](./#p=wang2025enhancing): "unfaithful trajectories that guess the correct option after a faulty chain of thought". [Li et al. 2024](./#p=li2025better) compare the CoT with a gold rationale, and [Mohammadi et al. 2025](./#p=mohammadi2025evaluating) check whether the reasoning "logically entails the reference explanation". Other papers exclude correctness: "the evaluation of faithfulness should not involve human-provided gold labels" ([Dehghanighobadi et al. 2025](./#p=dehghanighobadi2025can), after [Jacovi & Goldberg 2020](./#p=jacovi2020towards)), and the two outputs "should output the same value for any strategy $S$ , regardless of whether the answer is correct, since CIA measures faithfulness rather than correctness" ([Hong et al. 2026](./#p=hong2026making)). On the stricter reading, a trace that accurately reports flawed reasoning is faithful, while the correctness-based definitions count it as unfaithful whenever the answer happens to be right.

## Counterfactual

The corpus holds 129 definitions in the counterfactual family, from 73 of the 217 papers. By kind, 59 are informal, 31 operational, 22 formal, 13 criteria and 4 taxonomies. By stance, 70 are proposed, 44 adopted and 15 adapted. Sixteen of the 129 are companion terms, such as validity, minimality or simulation precision, rather than definitions of "counterfactual" itself. Nearly every entry describes a paired run: the model is run on an original and on a variant that differs from it in one specified respect, and the two outputs are compared. The eight clusters below differ on three points. The first is what is varied: the input, the model's own reasoning, or an internal state. The second is whether the variant must change the output to count as a counterfactual. The third is what the comparison is used to judge: an explanation, the model's reliance on a feature, or a training target.

In the tables, text in quotation marks is copied from the paper, with omissions marked by an ellipsis. Text without quotation marks is the extractor's gloss.

### Counterfactual explanation: an edited input that changes the decision

This is the explainable-AI sense, taken from Wachter et al. 2017. A counterfactual is an alternative input that would change the model's decision, and that alternative input is itself the explanation. In the LLM papers the model writes the alternative input itself, which gives a self-generated counterfactual explanation (SCE). An SCE is judged on two properties. Validity asks whether the model's own prediction changes when the edited input is fed back, usually in a fresh context. Minimality asks how close the edit is to the original. Three entries use "counterfactual explanation" for something else: a verbal what-if statement about how the output would change, rather than the edited input ([Agarwal et al. 2024](./#p=agarwal2024faithfulness), [Guo et al. 2026](./#p=guo2026introspective), [Pres et al. 2026a](./#p=pres2026consistency)). [Madsen et al. 2024](./#p=madsen2024self) call a valid counterfactual "faithful". That is the only explicit link from this cluster to [faithfulness](#faithfulness). 21 entries from 10 papers (9 proposed, 11 adopted, 1 adapted).

> A counterfactual explanation $\mathbf{x}_{\text{CE}}$ is a modified version of the original input $\mathbf{x}$ that would lead the model to change its decision, that is, $f(\mathbf{x})\neq f(\mathbf{x}_{\text{CE}})$ .
>
> ([Dehghanighobadi et al. 2025](./#p=dehghanighobadi2025can), §3.1 (Step 2), after Wachter et al. 2017)

### Counterfactual simulatability: an explanation that predicts behaviour on other inputs

Here the counterfactual is not the explanation but the test input. An explanation given on $x$ is good to the extent that an observer who reads it, human or model, can predict the model's output on other inputs $x'$. [Chen et al. 2023](./#p=pmlr-v235-chen24bl) give the definition and two metrics, precision and generality. Both are computed over the counterfactuals the observer judges simulatable. A later group narrows the observer's task to predicting the effect of a named edit. [Karvonen et al. 2026](./#p=karvonen2026would) and [Anonymous 2026d](./#p=anon2026trainingoversight) ask whether a specific prompt edit changes a behaviour. [Zeng et al. 2026](./#p=zeng2026evaluating) ask whether a perturbation changes the model's own majority answer. The entries disagree about what the notion measures: faithfulness, plausibility or usefulness (see Tensions). 21 entries from 14 papers (6 proposed, 11 adopted, 4 adapted).

> we propose to evaluate counterfactual simulatability of natural language explanations: whether an explanation can enable humans to precisely infer the model’s outputs on diverse counterfactuals of the explained input.
>
> ([Chen et al. 2023](./#p=pmlr-v235-chen24bl), Abstract)

- Simulation precision: $\frac{1}{|C^*|}\sum_{x'\in C^*}\mathbf{1}[h_{e}(x')=o_{x'}]$ over the simulatable counterfactuals $C^*$.
- Simulation generality: one minus the mean pairwise similarity $\alpha$ (BLEU, cosine or Jaccard) among the simulatable counterfactuals.
- Gain over a no-explanation baseline: $\Delta\mathrm{OverallAcc}=\mathrm{OverallAcc}(P_2)-\mathrm{OverallAcc}(P_1)$, where $P_2$ includes the self-explanation and $P_1$ does not ([Hong & Roth 2026](./#p=hong2026llm)).
- Counterfactual claim label ([Karvonen et al. 2026](./#p=karvonen2026would)): “A claim is true if its edit moved the behavior rate by at least 50 percentage points and false if it moved the rate by at most 15.”

### The counterfactual set: which inputs simulatability is tested on

Seven entries define the set of inputs on which an explanation is tested in cluster 2. They give four different answers:
- related questions that an LLM generates from the explanation ([Chen et al. 2023](./#p=pmlr-v235-chen24bl));
- the input with a planted cue removed, or LLM-generated questions rejection-sampled so that the simulator and the model disagree ([Hase & Potts 2026](./#p=hase2026counterfactual));
- minimal what-if variations organised by the Gricean maxims ([Hong & Roth 2026](./#p=hong2026llm));
- real dataset rows within a small Hamming distance of the original ([Mayne et al. 2026](./#p=mayne2026positivecasefaithfulnessllm), [Lewis-Lim et al. 2026](./#p=lewislim2026efficient)).

[Chen et al. 2023](./#p=pmlr-v235-chen24bl) note that the scores depend on this distribution. 7 entries from 5 papers (5 proposed, 2 adopted).

> For a question $x$ , we construct a set of counterfactuals $C(x)$ : inputs that are similar to $x$ , but differ in some way, such that a faithful explanation of the reference model’s behavior on $x$ should help predict its behavior across $C(x)$ .
>
> ([Mayne et al. 2026](./#p=mayne2026positivecasefaithfulnessllm), §2.2)

### Input intervention: the same input with one cue or factor changed

This is the largest cluster. A counterfactual is the original input with one specified element removed, inserted or replaced, everything else held fixed. A difference between the two outputs is read as the causal influence of that element. What is changed varies from paper to paper: a planted hint or biasing feature; a protected attribute such as nationality, profession or user background; a structured factor or concept; an incentive in the environment; or an earlier turn of a conversation. Of the 27 entries, 19 are proposed, so most papers build their own variant. Two entries state the intervention in Pearl's do-notation ([Matton et al. 2025](./#p=matton2025walk), [Anonymous 2026c](./#p=anon2026improving)). The entry from [Haskins et al. 2026](./#p=haskins2026training) is the one in which the counterfactual is not run: an LLM judge only imagines it. 27 entries from 21 papers (19 proposed, 3 adopted, 5 adapted).

### The counterfactual test of explanations: does the explanation mention the edit?

These entries use the same counterfactual as cluster 4, an input edit, but they judge the explanation rather than the model's reliance on the edited element. The procedure follows Atanasova et al. 2023. Words are inserted into the input. If the prediction changes and the explanation for the edited instance does not mention the inserted words, the explanation is counted as unfaithful. Variants replace the inserted words with LLM-generated modifiers ([Wang & Atanasova 2025](./#p=wang2025selfcritique)), with hint keywords ([Alon et al. 2026](./#p=alon2026faithfulness)) or with paired concept edits ([Arcuschin et al. 2026](./#p=arcuschin2026biases)). The Correlational Counterfactual Test of Siegel et al. 2024 weights each instance by the shift in the label distribution. No entry proposes the test, and its source is not in the map. 11 entries from 10 papers (9 adopted, 2 adapted).

> The counterfactual test works by making an intervention to the original instance to get an intervened instance. The evaluation then consists of two steps: (1) Identify counter instances: intervened instances whose prediction changes compared to the original instance. (2) Identify unfaithful instances: counter instances whose NLEs (generated by baseline methods or SR-NLE) do not contain the intervened word (determined by string matching).
>
> ([Wang & Atanasova 2025](./#p=wang2025selfcritique), §4.3. Atanasova is a co-author of this paper.)

### Intervention on the reasoning: an edited, truncated or resampled chain of thought

The counterfactual is a version of the model's own intermediate output, usually the chain of thought, with the input held fixed. The edits include:
- replacing a step with a contradiction;
- swapping an operator;
- altering the conclusion;
- substituting an irrelevant fact;
- truncating the trace;
- resampling a sentence to different content.

The same idea is applied to other intermediates: a generated mediator ([Somov et al. 2026](./#p=somov2026breaking)), a stated plan ([Fareed 2026](./#p=fareed2026plan)), and a rationale span read by a consistency head ([Ananthula & Kumarappan 2026](./#p=ananthula2026decodable)). If the answer follows the edit, the answer is taken to depend on the reasoning. [Akter et al. 2025](./#p=akter2025causal) call the resulting sensitivity a measure of [faithfulness](#faithfulness). 26 entries from 19 papers (19 proposed, 6 adopted, 1 adapted).

>
> ([Han et al. 2026](./#p=han2026rfeval), §3.1)

### Counterfactual training pairs

Nine entries use counterfactuals as training data rather than as a test. The counterfactual is a rationale or context, paired with the output the model should give on it. The training goes in one of two opposite directions:
- Sensitivity: the model is trained to follow a rationale that leads to a different answer ([Wang et al. 2023](./#p=wang2023scott), [Paul et al. 2024](./#p=paul2024making), [Wang et al. 2026](./#p=wang2026case), [Akter et al. 2025](./#p=akter2025causal)), or to lower its confidence when the rationale is perturbed ([Wang et al. 2022](./#p=wang2023pinto)).
- Invariance: the model is trained to give, under a biasing or pressuring context, the response it gives without that context ([Chua et al. 2024](./#p=chua2024biasaugmented), [Tang & Khanna 2026](./#p=tang2026unlearning)).

- Activation patching: $M(x;h(x)\leftarrow h(x'))$ ([Li et al. 2025b](./#p=li2025training)).
- Adversarial attention: the largest JSD from the original attention $\hat\alpha$ among weights that keep the output TVD $\le\epsilon$, with $\epsilon=0.01$ for classification and $0.05$ for QA ([Jain & Wallace 2019](./#p=jain2019attention)).

Four kinds of drift appear as the definitions are passed on.

1. **Simulatability changes status.** [Chen et al. 2023](./#p=pmlr-v235-chen24bl) ask whether an explanation lets humans “precisely infer the model’s outputs on diverse counterfactuals of the explained input”. [Turpin et al. 2023](./#p=turpin2023language) call this “The counterfactual simulatability framework of explanation faithfulness”. In [Turpin et al. 2025](./#p=turpin2025teaching) it becomes a biconditional: “an explanation is faithful if and only if the explanation allows a human or model to accurately predict how the model will behave on other relevant inputs”. [Hong & Roth 2026](./#p=hong2026llm) change what is predicted, to “how a model’s prediction would change on counterfactual inputs”. [Karvonen et al. 2026](./#p=karvonen2026would) change the counterfactuals: Chen et al. are said to argue that “a good explanation should help an observer predict behavior under counterfactual edits”. Three things shift along the way. The observer goes from humans to “a human or model”. The counterfactuals go from LLM-generated related questions to edits. And the notion goes from a metric that Chen et al. call a special case of faithfulness to a necessary and sufficient condition for it.
2. **The counterfactual explanation acquires a target and changes author.** In [Dehghanighobadi et al. 2025](./#p=dehghanighobadi2025can), after Wachter et al., it is an input that “would lead the model to change its decision”. [Mayne et al. 2025](./#p=mayne2025llms) tie it to “a specific alternative output”, and [Madsen et al. 2024](./#p=madsen2024self) to “the opposite prediction”. The edit is then no longer found by an optimiser. [Dehghanighobadi et al. 2025](./#p=dehghanighobadi2025can) write that “we do not solve an optimization problem and instead ask the model itself to generate the counterfactual explanation”. [Zeng et al. 2026](./#p=zeng2026evaluating) recast it as “the propose-and-verify form of Edit-Proposal”.
3. **The counterfactual test widens.** [Parcalabescu & Frank 2023](./#p=parcalabescu2023measuring) describe Atanasova et al.'s trained helper model that inserts words. Later papers widen the test step by step. [Gema et al. 2026](./#p=gema2026chainofthought) describe it as testing explanations “through input edits, reconstruction, or output-distribution shifts”. [Bhan et al. 2025](./#p=bhan2025did) have “auxiliary models to generate counterfactual texts designed to change the LLM outcome”. [Cheah et al. 2026](./#p=cheah2026training) use controlled interventions “such as the introduction of biasing hints”. By the end, the label "counterfactual test" also covers Turpin-style hint pairs, which [Turpin et al. 2023](./#p=turpin2023language) had filed under counterfactual simulatability.
4. **Borrowers add the word.** [Lanham et al. 2023](./#p=lanham2023measuring) use "counterfactual" once in the whole text, and only for a cited work. Their truncation test reappears as "prefix counterfactuals" in [Parekh & Goyal 2026](./#p=parekh2026drop). [Chen et al. 2025](./#p=chen2025reasoning) use the word only in related work and in a reference title, yet [Alon et al. 2026](./#p=alon2026faithfulness) apply it to the same hint-flip filter: “we regard this as a counterfactual”.

### Attribution check

Thirty adopted or adapted entries, from 25 papers, cite at least one source that is in the map. Each was compared with the source paper's own entries. The source text was then opened for eight sources, covering 11 borrowings. (2020)". The nearest statement in Hase et al. is their general definition, "A model is simulatable to the extent that an observer, or simulator, can predict its outputs." Chen et al. cite a different paper, Hase & Bansal 2020, for the "long history" of counterfactual simulatability.
2. [Mayne et al. 2026](./#p=mayne2026positivecasefaithfulnessllm) #6, citing [Hase et al. 2020](./#p=hase2020leakageadjusted). 3. [Shen et al. 2026](./#p=shen2026detecting) #6, citing [Turpin et al. 2023](./#p=turpin2023language). Shen et al. cite Turpin et al. The nearest statement is their pair of input-feature counterfactuals (cluster 4). [Lanham et al. 2023](./#p=lanham2023measuring), cited in the same place, does edit the CoT.
4. [Li et al. 2025a](./#p=li2025drift) #4, citing [Lanham et al. 2023](./#p=lanham2023measuring). **Partial mismatches**

5. [Turpin et al. 2025](./#p=turpin2025teaching) #2, citing [Chen et al. 2023](./#p=pmlr-v235-chen24bl), Doshi-Velez & Kim 2017 and Hase & Bansal 2020. Chen et al. state no "if and only if". ### Tensions

1. **Must the variant change the output?**
   - Only flips count: the counterfactual explanation is defined by a flip ([Dehghanighobadi et al. 2025](./#p=dehghanighobadi2025can): $f(\mathbf{x})\neq f(\mathbf{x}_{\text{CE}})$; [Mayne et al. 2025](./#p=mayne2025llms): “would flip the LLM’s decision”). The counterfactual test keeps only “intervened instances whose prediction changes compared to the original instance” ([Wang & Atanasova 2025](./#p=wang2025selfcritique)). [Alon et al. 2026](./#p=alon2026faithfulness) call a pair a counterfactual only when $a_0\neq a_1=\text{hint}$.
   - Non-flipping variants are wanted: [Zaman & Srivastava 2025a](./#p=zaman2025causal) build counterfactuals that “yield the same answer but are based on different reasoning”. [Jain & Wallace 2019](./#p=jain2019attention) define adversarial attention by “an equivalent prediction”. [Shiromani et al. 2026](./#p=shiromani2026necessary) keep only traces that end in the same final answer type.
   - Most intervention entries ([Matton et al. 2025](./#p=matton2025walk), [Zhang et al. 2026](./#p=zhang2026audit), [Cheah et al. 2026](./#p=cheah2026training)) define the counterfactual by the edit and treat a flip as an outcome to measure.

   For the first group a non-flipping edit is a failed counterfactual; for the second it is the object of study.
2. **Remove the cue or swap it?** [Hase & Potts 2026](./#p=hase2026counterfactual) (“The counterfactual input is the input without the cue.”), [Guo et al. 2026](./#p=guo2026introspective) ($x_{\setminus C}$), [Karvonen et al. 2026](./#p=karvonen2026would) (“Ground truth comes from removing the cue”), [Ferreira et al. 2026](./#p=ferreira2026outofsight) and [Ferreira et al. 2025](./#p=ferreira2026truthful) compare against the input without the cue. [Shiromani et al. 2026](./#p=shiromani2026necessary) reject that comparison: “Comparing W to a no-hint prompt would confound the hint’s content with its presence”. For them, "the hint caused the answer" means that the answer changes when $h$ is swapped for $h'$. [Li 2026](./#p=li2026monitorability) ask for both no-hint and alternate-hint runs. A given answer can then count as caused by the hint under removal and not under a swap.
3. **Is a single flip evidence of causation?** [Alon et al. 2026](./#p=alon2026faithfulness) treat each flip as “evidence that the hint influenced the model’s decision making”. [Ferreira et al. 2026](./#p=ferreira2026outofsight) use the greedy no-hint prediction as a per-example label. [Anonymous 2026c](./#p=anon2026improving) hold the opposite view: “detecting intervention-caused outcomes is a counterfactual target”. A unit's outcome without the intervention is never observed and can only be bounded. [Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring) likewise score monitors against a lower bound on the share of behaviour attributable to the intervention, not against per-example labels (see [monitorability](#monitorability)). [Shen et al. 2026](./#p=shen2026detecting) say that counterfactual CoT interventions reveal causal strength across a population, not the faithfulness of any one trace. [Karvonen et al. 2026](./#p=karvonen2026would) take a middle course: they resample and label a claim true or false only when the behaviour rate moves by at least 50 points or at most 15.
4. **Keep the rest of the trace fixed, or regenerate it?** [Cox et al. 2026](./#p=cox2026posthoc) substitute the whole CoT. [Han et al. 2026](./#p=han2026rfeval) insert flawed reasoning while holding the input fixed. [Khanzadeh 2026](./#p=khanzadeh2026project) re-execute from the edited step, so later steps are re-sampled and a self-correction can hide the dependence. [Macar et al. 2025](./#p=macar2025thought) argue that resampling one sentence understates its effect: “Counterfactual importance evaluates a sentence’s immediate impact but misses resilience”. For this reason they measure importance on rollouts in which the sentence's content never reappears. A step that has no effect when the trace is re-executed may be decisive when it is substituted.
5. **Which inputs count as counterfactuals for simulatability?** The papers give four answers:
   - minimal edits ([Hong & Roth 2026](./#p=hong2026llm): “minimal what-if variations of an input”);
   - “any simulatable counterfactual in the natural language distribution” ([Chen et al. 2023](./#p=pmlr-v235-chen24bl));
   - real rows “rather than synthetic perturbations” ([Lewis-Lim et al. 2026](./#p=lewislim2026efficient), [Mayne et al. 2026](./#p=mayne2026positivecasefaithfulnessllm));
   - questions rejection-sampled so that simulator and model disagree ([Hase & Potts 2026](./#p=hase2026counterfactual)).

   Precision is averaged over this set, so scores computed on different sets cannot be compared.
6. **What does counterfactual simulatability measure?**
   - Faithfulness: [Turpin et al. 2023](./#p=turpin2023language), [Turpin et al. 2025](./#p=turpin2025teaching) (the "if and only if"), [Arcuschin et al. 2025](./#p=arcuschin2025chain) and [Mayne et al. 2026](./#p=mayne2026positivecasefaithfulnessllm).
   - Something else: [Agarwal et al. 2024](./#p=agarwal2024faithfulness) file it under plausibility-oriented evaluation. [Hong & Roth 2026](./#p=hong2026llm) treat it as usefulness. [Karvonen et al. 2026](./#p=karvonen2026would) define an explanation as “a behavioral claim testable by counterfactual experiments, not an explanation of the model’s internal computation”.
   - In between: [Chen et al. 2023](./#p=pmlr-v235-chen24bl) call it a special case of faithfulness that is not necessary for it.

   The [faithfulness](#faithfulness) section covers the faithfulness side.
7. **Does the counterfactual test measure faithfulness?**
   - Yes: [Wang & Atanasova 2025](./#p=wang2025selfcritique), [Alon et al. 2026](./#p=alon2026faithfulness), [Cheah et al. 2026](./#p=cheah2026training), [Zaman & Srivastava 2025b](./#p=zaman2025chainofthought) and [Gema et al. 2026](./#p=gema2026chainofthought) use it as a faithfulness test.
   - No: [Parcalabescu & Frank 2023](./#p=parcalabescu2023measuring) reclassify it as a self-consistency test. [Bhan et al. 2025](./#p=bhan2025did) reject it as black-box and input-output only. [Zaman & Srivastava 2025a](./#p=zaman2025causal) leave it out because their framework cannot evaluate it. [Matton et al. 2025](./#p=matton2025walk) object that it judges the explanation of the counterfactual question, not of the original.
8. **Attention counterfactuals.** [Jain & Wallace 2019](./#p=jain2019attention) take the existence of an attention distribution that is “maximally distinct” from the original but gives the same prediction as evidence that attention is not explanation. [Wiegreffe & Pinter 2019](./#p=wiegreffe2019attention) accept the counterfactual framework only “as a concept”. They object that "due to the per-instance nature of the demonstration and the fact that model parameters have not been learned or manipulated directly, Jain and Wallace have not shown the existence of an adversarial model that produces the claimed adversarial distributions." On their reading, an alternative attention distribution is a counterfactual only if a trained model can actually produce it.

## Monitorability

The extraction holds 328 definitions in the monitorability family, taken from 82 of the 217 papers. By kind, 92 are informal, 64 operational, 59 failure-mode, 42 criterion, 38 taxonomy and 33 formal; by stance, 196 are proposed, 104 adopted and 28 adapted. Only 108 entries, from 40 papers, have a term that names monitorability or a monitorable object; the remaining 220 define companion notions. The picture in one sentence: most definitions make monitorability a relation between a model and a reader: whether a given monitor can predict a specified property of the model's behaviour from what the model exposes ([Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring), [Baker et al. 2025](./#p=baker2025monitoring)), while a second group makes it a property of the trace alone, its faithful and complete verbalization of what drove the answer, often citing the same sources for that stronger reading. The nine clusters below place every entry in exactly one cluster. The first two belong together: the first holds what monitorability is in the predictive sense, the second the scores and evaluation designs that measure it, and they are kept apart because the literature cites them separately.

### Monitorability as a monitor's ability to predict a target property

30 entries from 22 papers (21 informal, 6 criterion, 2 formal, 1 taxonomy; 15 adopted, 13 proposed, 2 adapted).

Monitorability here is relative to a property and to a reader. A model is monitorable for property $Y$ by monitor $M$ to the degree that $M$, reading what the model exposes (CoT, actions or, in some entries, activations), predicts $Y$. The cluster's defining move is negative: the CoT need not reproduce the model's internal computation. Several entries say this explicitly and set the definition against faithfulness (see the [Faithfulness](#faithfulness) section).

> We develop evaluations for monitorability, which we define as a monitor’s ability to accurately predict a specified property of an agent’s actions, e.g. whether the model is acting or will act harmfully.
>
> ([Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring), §2)

**Formal versions.**
- [Anonymous 2026 (implementation)](./#p=anon2026implementation), Informal Def. 1: property $P$ of actor $A$ is monitorable on task distribution $D_t$ with respect to monitor $M$ if $P(A,t,\pi)\approx M(\pi)$ with high probability over $t\sim D_t$, $\pi\sim A(t)$. Here $P$ may depend on the actor and the task, not only on the transcript $\pi$.
- [Anwar et al. 2026](./#p=anwar2026analyzing), Def. 3.6 and Props. 3.7, 3.10: the uplift from reading the CoT $Z$ is $\delta := \alpha_{\text{CoT}}-\alpha_{\text{Prompt}}$, the accuracy gain of the Bayes-optimal CoT monitor over the Bayes-optimal prompt-only monitor. - [Baker et al. 2025](./#p=baker2025monitoring) connects the definition to faithfulness by redefining the latter. A latent $z$ is faithful with respect to $v$ if $z$ is a sufficient statistic for $v$, and a monitor that predicts $v$ from $z$ shows that $z$ is faithful with respect to $v$ and legible to it (see Attribution check, item 2).

### Scoring monitorability: metrics and evaluation designs

55 entries from 31 papers (27 operational, 13 formal, 12 taxonomy, 2 informal, 1 criterion; 36 proposed, 12 adopted, 7 adapted).

This cluster operationalizes the previous one. Monitorability becomes the agreement between a monitor's verdict $Z$ and a ground-truth label for the target property, summarized by a statistic. The entries differ in three choices: how the label is produced (an input intervention such as a hint, a known solution process, or a verifiable outcome), which statistic is used (g-mean², AUROC, binned MSE, detection or catch rate), and the regime (default or adversarial). Where the label comes from an intervention, the score is tied to a counterfactual contrast between the answer with and without the intervention (see the [Counterfactual](#counterfactual) section). [Hase & Potts 2026](./#p=hase2026counterfactual) states the link directly, treating monitoring as counterfactual simulation.

> A natural choice is the geometric mean of sensitivity (true positive rate) and specificity (true negative rate), $\text{g-mean}=\sqrt{\mathrm{TPR}\cdot\mathrm{TNR}}$ [25]. We square this quantity to more sharply penalize failures, yielding a monitorability measure which is both more conservative and more immediately interpretable
>
> ([Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring), §2.1)

**Formal versions.**
- [Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring): $\text{g-mean}^2=\mathrm{TPR}\cdot\mathrm{TNR}$; a random monitor scores 0.25. For intervention evaluations, the true positive rate is corrected for answers the intervention did not cause:
  $$\mathrm{TPR}_{mc}=\min\Big\{1,\ \frac{\Pr(Z=1\mid X=1,Y=1)}{R_{\text{effect}}}\Big\},\qquad R_{\text{effect}}=\frac{\mathrm{TE}}{\Pr(Y=1\mid X=1)},\qquad \text{g-mean}^2_{\text{int}}=\mathrm{TPR}_{mc}\sqrt{\mathrm{TNR}_{X=1}\,\mathrm{TNR}_{X=0}}.$$
- [Anonymous 2026 (improving monitorability)](./#p=anon2026improving): causal recall $r_c:=\Pr(Z(1)=1\mid U=1)$ with $U:=\mathbb{1}\{Y(1)=1,\,Y(0)=0\}$. With $R=(p_1-p_0)/p_1$, the observable bounds are $\max\{0,(\mathrm{TPR}_{ob}+R-1)/R\}\le r_c\le\min\{1,\mathrm{TPR}_{ob}/R\}$. The upper end is Guan's $\mathrm{TPR}_{mc}$, which bounds causal recall from above and does not identify it (Theorem 1).
- [Wang et al. 2026](./#p=wang2026monitorbench): $\min\{\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X=1,Y=1]/R_{\text{effect}},\,1\}$, the same form applied to flag rates on outcome and process tasks.
- [Bürger et al. 2026](./#p=burger2026chain): $M_0=\min(1,\pi_Z/R)$ with $R=\max(0,p_{11}-p_{01})/p_{11}$. Here $\pi_Z$ is the rate at which the CoT *articulates* the attribute, not a monitor's flag rate. $M_1$ is the same score restricted to cases where the CoT is necessary ($p_{10}<0.01$).
- [Anonymous 2026 (implementation)](./#p=anon2026implementation): $\hat\mu=s\big(M(\pi),\hat P(e(A),t_{\hat P},\pi)\big)\in[0,1]$, the agreement of monitor score and a misbehaviour proxy on elicited transcripts. The default statistic is the binned MSE $\mathrm{BMSE}(X,Y)=\operatorname{mean}_b\,\mathbb{E}[(X-Y)^2\mid Y\in b]$ over non-empty bins $[0,\tfrac12]$ and $(\tfrac12,1]$ (lower is more monitorable). The paired variant is $s(\Delta M,\Delta\hat P)$.
- [Yang et al. 2025](./#p=yang2025investigating): false negatives $\Pr(M=0\mid Y_{\text{cue}}=1)$ and false positives $\Pr(M=1\mid Y_{\text{cue}}=0)$, estimated as $\mathrm{EEMR}=(1-\mathrm{MFR})\cdot\mathrm{CIR}_a$ and $\mathrm{OSM}=\mathrm{MFR}\cdot\mathrm{AKR}_a$.
- [Hase & Potts 2026](./#p=hase2026counterfactual) uses the unsquared $\sqrt{\mathrm{TPR}\cdot(1-\mathrm{FPR})}$; [MacDermott et al. 2025](./#p=macdermott2025reasoning) trains on a reward of $+1$ when the monitor predicts the latent correctly and $-1$ otherwise, reweighted by inverse class frequency; [Anwar et al. 2026](./#p=anwar2026analyzing) trains on $I(O;Z\mid X)$ as a label-free proxy for $I(Y;Z\mid X)$.

### Monitorability as faithful and complete verbalization

38 entries from 23 papers (14 criterion, 11 informal, 6 operational, 3 taxonomy, 2 failure-mode, 2 formal; 24 proposed, 12 adopted, 2 adapted).

Here monitorability is a property of the trace itself. The CoT must state the factors and steps that produced the answer, and monitoring presupposes faithfulness (see the [Faithfulness](#faithfulness) section, which these definitions invoke explicitly). Within the cluster, the entries differ on how much must be verbalized. Some require faithfulness plus completeness ([Meek et al. 2025](./#p=meek2025measuring): faithfulness and verbosity). Some require faithfulness plus legibility ([Xiong et al. 2026](./#p=xiong2026monitorability), [Google DeepMind 2026](./#p=google2026gemini4argon)). Others require only the monitored property or the key influences ([Anonymous 2026 (CoT reliance)](./#p=anon2026chainofthought), [Bürger et al. 2026](./#p=burger2026chain), [Kaufmann et al. 2026](./#p=kaufmann2026aligned)). Two entries locate monitorability in the model alone, independent of any monitor ([Xiong et al. 2026](./#p=xiong2026monitorability), [Yang et al. 2025](./#p=yang2025investigating)).

> This visibility could help us spot unsafe or misaligned behavior (monitorability), but only if the CoT is transparent about its internal reasoning (faithfulness).
>
> ([Meek et al. 2025](./#p=meek2025measuring), Abstract)

**Formal versions.**
- [Meek et al. 2025](./#p=meek2025measuring): monitorability $=\tfrac12(F+V)$. $F$ is the cue-acknowledgment rate and $V$ the fraction of LLM-panel causal factors present in the uncued CoT. The paper notes that a weighted mean, geometric mean or minimum could replace the average.
- [Bürger et al. 2026](./#p=burger2026chain), Eq. 3: a monitorable CoT satisfies $\pi_Z\ge\mathrm{KR}$, where $\pi_Z$ is the articulation rate and $\mathrm{KR}$ the unobservable key-influence rate. Since $R\le\mathrm{KR}$, the observable test is $\pi_Z\ge R$.
- [Kaufmann et al. 2026](./#p=kaufmann2026aligned), Defs. C.1–C.2: a CoT on input $I$ is monitorable for monitor $M$ and task $T$ iff $f^M_T\big(M_{\text{sem}}(\mathrm{CoT})\big)=f^M_T\big(\pi_{\text{sem}}(\mathrm{CoT})\big)$. That is, the monitor classifies the text as it would under the model's own semantics. The monitorability of a policy is $\mathbb{E}_{I\sim\rho}\,\mathbb{1}\big[f^M_T(M_{\text{sem}}(\mathrm{CoT}_I))=f^M_T(\pi_{\text{sem}}(\mathrm{CoT}_I))\big]$.

### Necessity and causal dependence of the CoT

25 entries from 16 papers (10 criterion, 5 informal, 4 operational, 3 failure-mode, 2 taxonomy, 1 formal; 16 proposed, 8 adopted, 1 adapted).

These entries define the condition under which a CoT is expected to be monitorable, not monitorability itself. The first version is necessity, a condition on the task: the model cannot succeed without the CoT. [Korbak et al. 2025](./#p=korbak2025chain) separates it from the mere propensity to think out loud. The second version is causal dependence, a condition on the trace: the answer must depend on what the CoT says. [Skaf et al. 2025](./#p=skaf2025large)'s load-bearing CoT, [Jia & Mu 2026](./#p=jia2026decorative) and [Anonymous 2026 (CRISP)](./#p=anon2026crisp) take this route. [Fareed 2026](./#p=fareed2026plan) applies it to agents' stated plans.

> Necessity: We say CoT is necessary for a task if the model cannot succeed without it. This condition formalizes the requirement that a model must “think out loud.”
>
> ([Emmons et al. 2025 (necessity)](./#p=emmons2025chain), §2)

**Formal versions.** [Bürger et al. 2026](./#p=burger2026chain), Def. 1: the CoT is necessary for the target answer if $p_{10}<0.01$, the no-CoT probability of that answer. Its hierarchy then runs Level 0 (not necessary), Level 1 (necessary for the answer), Level 2 (necessary to process property $P$) and Level 3.$m$ ($m$ consecutive forward passes dedicated to $P$). [Anonymous 2026 (CoT reliance)](./#p=anon2026chainofthought) replaces the threshold with a graded reliance $\mathrm{acc}_{\text{CoT}}-\mathrm{acc}_{\text{no-CoT}}$ per (model, task) pair. [Fareed 2026](./#p=fareed2026plan)'s binding rate is the frequency with which the realized irreversible write matches the seeded plan; zero variance across seeds marks a causally inert plan.

### Legibility and coverage: a reader can follow, check or reconstruct the reasoning

52 entries from 19 papers (14 operational, 12 informal, 10 failure-mode, 7 formal, 5 taxonomy, 4 criterion; 31 proposed, 13 adopted, 8 adapted).

Legibility is a relation between the trace and a named reader. The reader is a time-limited human or a verifier in [Kirchner et al. 2024](./#p=kirchner2024proververifier), a human with the user's language in [Emmons et al. 2025 (pragmatic)](./#p=emmons2025pragmatic), another model in [Roytburg et al. 2026](./#p=roytburg2026measuring) (a weaker one), [Jiao et al. 2026](./#p=jiao2026tandem) (the frozen pre-RL junior) and [Sullivan & Koller 2026](./#p=sullivan2026language) (instruct models, and peers trained the same way), and the monitor itself in [Baker et al. 2025](./#p=baker2025monitoring). Coverage and its relatives (transfer utility, trace legibility, sufficiency of reasoning, the information-sufficiency gate in [Mao et al. 2026](./#p=mao2026taming)) ask whether that reader can reach the answer from the trace. The failure modes are illegibility, language drift and compression. [Kirchner et al. 2024](./#p=kirchner2024proververifier) and [Jose 2025](./#p=jose2025reasoning) define legibility as independent of how the model actually reasoned.

> One way to increase confidence in the outputs of Large Language Models (LLMs) is to support them with reasoning that is clear and easy to check — a property we call legibility.
>
> ([Kirchner et al. 2024](./#p=kirchner2024proververifier), Abstract)

**Formal versions.**
- [Kirchner et al. 2024](./#p=kirchner2024proververifier), §3.1: prover $\pi$ is verifiable to $v$ iff completeness holds, $\forall x:\ \mathrm{correct}(x,\pi(x))=v(x,\pi(x))=1$, and soundness holds, $\forall\pi'\in\mathcal H,\ \forall x:\ \mathrm{correct}(x,\pi'(x))=0\Rightarrow v(x,\pi'(x))=0$. [Kim & Lee 2026](./#p=kim2026mitigating) drops correctness from completeness: correct $\Rightarrow v(x,\pi(x))=1$.
- [Sullivan & Koller 2026](./#p=sullivan2026language), Def. 1: $LD(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_x\big[\mathrm{KL}\big(P^{(HL)}(\cdot\mid x)\,\big\Vert\,\pi(\cdot\mid x)\big)\big]$ over prompts from the human-language marginal.
- [Jiao et al. 2026](./#p=jiao2026tandem): junior legibility is the junior's mean per-token cross-entropy on the senior's CoT, and the overlap $\alpha=\sum_v\min\big(p_{\text{sen}}(v\mid\cdot),\,p_{\text{jun}}(v\mid\cdot)\big)$ averaged over positions.
- [Roytburg et al. 2026](./#p=roytburg2026measuring): $\mathrm{FOTU}(T)$ is the mean over prefix bins $x$ of $\mu_T(x)$, the weak model's accuracy given $x\%$ of the trace. SOTU is the normalized entropy of the bin where the weak model first answers correctly, and the regression rate is the fraction of consecutive prefixes where weak-model correctness drops.
- [Yu et al. 2026](./#p=yu2026outcome): reasoning is sufficient if $p_\theta(\cdot\mid q,t')\approx p_\theta(\cdot\mid t')$ for the answer-stripped CoT $t'$.

### Encoded reasoning and steganography

35 entries from 17 papers (19 failure-mode, 7 informal, 4 operational, 3 criterion, 1 taxonomy, 1 formal; 21 proposed, 13 adopted, 1 adapted).

The CoT carries information that the model uses but a reader cannot recover. The information may be hidden in word choice or syntax (lexical schemes), in patterns of reasoning steps ([Zhou & May 2026](./#p=zhou2026conceptual)), or in a channel between models ([Mathew et al. 2024](./#p=mathew2025hidden); [Skaf et al. 2025](./#p=skaf2025large) separates this steganographic communication from steganographic reasoning). The entries agree that the hidden content must be used by the model: [Skaf et al. 2025](./#p=skaf2025large) requires load-bearing tokens, and [Roger 2025](./#p=anthropic2025paraphrases) tests use by paraphrase distillation. They disagree on who the content is hidden from (see Tensions).

> Encoded reasoning: The benefit from CoT may be attained by LLMs encoding the relevant information in the generated reasoning in a way that is not understandable to human readers (a form of steganography).
>
> ([Lanham et al. 2023](./#p=lanham2023measuring), §1)

**Formal versions.** [Roger & Greenblatt 2023](./#p=roger2023preventing) measures the capacity of the encoder–paraphraser–decoder channel as $I(M,\hat M)=H(M)-H(M\mid\hat M)$. It estimates this from the exact-decode rate $f$ on $n$-bit payloads as $C=n-f\log_2\frac1f-(1-f)\log_2\frac{2^n-1}{1-f}$. [Skaf et al. 2025](./#p=skaf2025large): a trace is steganographic with respect to a monitor if (a) it encodes harmful planning in load-bearing tokens and (b) the monitor can neither decode the encodings nor detect their presence. If the monitor detects the encodings without decoding them, the CoT is overtly encoded instead.

### Obfuscation, evasion and controllability

47 entries from 29 papers (24 failure-mode, 6 operational, 6 taxonomy, 5 informal, 3 formal, 3 criterion; 26 proposed, 17 adopted, 4 adapted).

In this cluster the misbehaviour continues while the trace stops revealing it to the monitor. The mechanisms named are omission, misleading framing, content-preserving rephrasing that fools only the LLM monitor ([Schulz 2026](./#p=schulz2026monitor)), changed internal representations that defeat a probe ([Taufeeque et al. 2026](./#p=taufeeque2026obfuscation)), and injected plans that the model adopts as its own ([Chidambaram et al. 2026](./#p=chidambaram2026corrupted)). The usual cause is optimization pressure, whether direct (a CoT monitor in the reward) or indirect (output-only supervision in [Drori et al. 2025](./#p=drori2025output), consistency training in [Imran et al. 2026](./#p=imran2026consistency)). Monitor awareness and instructions are also named. CoT controllability, the ability to shape one's own CoT under instruction, is treated as a precursor capability.

> such an objective could cause agents to learn to hide their intent within the CoT while continuing to exhibit misaligned behavior, a phenomenon we will call obfuscated reward hacking.
>
> ([Baker et al. 2025](./#p=baker2025monitoring), §1)

**Formal versions.** [Drori et al. 2025](./#p=drori2025output), Defs. 3, 5, 6. With $M_{\text{out}}=m(p,o)$ and $M_{\text{CoT}}=m(p,c)$, feedback spillover is $\mathbb{E}_\theta[M_{\text{CoT}}]<\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{\text{control}}}[M_{\text{CoT}}]$. Spillover is conditional if $\nabla_{\text{CoT}}\mathbb{E}[M_{\text{out}}]\cdot\nabla_{\text{CoT}}\mathbb{E}[M_{\text{CoT}}]>0$ (safe-looking CoTs make safe-looking outputs likelier) and parametric if $\nabla_{\text{out}}\mathbb{E}[M_{\text{out}}]\cdot\nabla_{\text{CoT}}\mathbb{E}[M_{\text{CoT}}]>0$ (updates that make outputs look safe also make CoTs look safe). [Taufeeque et al. 2026](./#p=taufeeque2026obfuscation) separates obfuscated policies from obfuscated activations with the probe-score decomposition $\psi(y,\theta_0)$ versus $\psi(y,\theta_0)-\psi(y,\theta)$.

### Tensions

**Does monitorability require faithfulness?** One side says it does not, or requires only a weaker condition. Its definitions are [Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring) ("does not require a model’s externalized reasoning to faithfully reflect its internal reasoning process"), [Baker et al. 2025](./#p=baker2025monitoring), [MacDermott et al. 2025](./#p=macdermott2025reasoning) ("even if that reasoning is unfaithful"), [Kaufmann et al. 2026](./#p=kaufmann2026aligned) and [Anonymous 2026 (CoT reliance)](./#p=anon2026chainofthought) ("monitorability remains an easier objective than faithfulness because of a weaker overall comprehensiveness requirement"). The other side requires it: [Meek et al. 2025](./#p=meek2025measuring) ("but only if the CoT is transparent about its internal reasoning (faithfulness)"), [Occhipinti et al. 2026](./#p=occhipinti2026probing), [Xiong et al. 2026](./#p=xiong2026monitorability), [Haskins et al. 2026](./#p=haskins2026training), [Bhupatiraju & Nyaupane 2026](./#p=bhupatiraju2026stated), [Anonymous 2026 (one draw)](./#p=anon2026one), [Jose 2025](./#p=jose2025reasoning), [Chen et al. 2025](./#p=chen2025reasoning), [Google DeepMind 2026](./#p=google2026gemini4argon) and [Golechha et al. 2026](./#p=golechha2026preliminary). A test case is a CoT that names the hint that changed the answer but gives a reconstructed account of how the answer was computed. For Guan and Baker it is monitorable for hint-reliance. For Xiong it is not ("faithfully reflect the model’s internal decision-making path"), nor for Bhupatiraju & Nyaupane ("reflects the computation that directly produces its answer"). Baker's property-relative faithfulness, a sufficient statistic for the monitored property, is the sense in which both sides could agree. However, the entries requiring faithfulness use either the reasoning-process sense or hint acknowledgment.

**Is monitorability a property of the model, or of the model and a reader?** Most definitions make it reader-relative: [Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring), [Emmons et al. 2025 (necessity)](./#p=emmons2025chain) ("monitorable to a reader (human or automated)"), [Anonymous 2026 (improving monitorability)](./#p=anon2026improving) ("depends on both the task model and the monitoring setup"), [Anonymous 2026 (implementation)](./#p=anon2026implementation), [Kaufmann et al. 2026](./#p=kaufmann2026aligned) and [Algaba et al. 2026](./#p=algaba2026reasoning) ("any monitorability claim, including for faithfulness, should specify both"). [Emmons et al. 2025 (pragmatic)](./#p=emmons2025pragmatic) measures it for a human monitor as a lower bound on general monitorability. On the other side, [Xiong et al. 2026](./#p=xiong2026monitorability) makes it "independent of the monitor’s specific capabilities", even though its g-mean² is computed for one monitor. [Yang et al. 2025](./#p=yang2025investigating) separates being monitorable (verbalization) from being monitored correctly (monitor reliability). [Meek et al. 2025](./#p=meek2025measuring)'s score contains no monitor term at all. On the reader-relative definitions, a CoT read by two monitors of different strength has two monitorability values; on Xiong's, it has one.

**Must the CoT be causally upstream of the answer, and is necessity enough?** [Jia & Mu 2026](./#p=jia2026decorative) ("monitoring is only meaningful if the written chain causally constrains the answer"), [Anonymous 2026 (CRISP)](./#p=anon2026crisp), [Fareed 2026](./#p=fareed2026plan) and [Xiong et al. 2026](./#p=xiong2026monitorability) ("without necessarily establishing a strong causal link between its reasoning and its answer") require causal dependence. [Baker et al. 2025](./#p=baker2025monitoring) rejects the requirement: "A CoT may perfectly describe the model’s latent reasoning without being causally upstream of the output." [Anwar et al. 2026](./#p=anwar2026analyzing) notes that post-hoc CoTs can satisfy its necessary condition $I(O;Z\mid X)>0$. On sufficiency, [Korbak et al. 2025](./#p=korbak2025chain) ("unable to complete the task without CoT") and [Emmons et al. 2025 (necessity)](./#p=emmons2025chain) argue that necessity enables detection. [Anonymous 2026 (CoT reliance)](./#p=anon2026chainofthought) finds that the claim fails for hint-usage monitoring, and [Bürger et al. 2026](./#p=burger2026chain) records monitorability failures among cases where the CoT is necessary. Consider a post-hoc CoT that accurately reports the hint: it is monitorable for Baker and decorative for Jia & Mu. Consider next a CoT that the task requires but that omits the hint: it satisfies Emmons's condition and fails Bürger's $M_1$.

**Encoded from whom?** [Lanham et al. 2023](./#p=lanham2023measuring) ("not understandable to human readers") and [Schulz 2026](./#p=schulz2026monitor) ("This does not fit the definition of encoded reasoning, as the reasoning is not hidden from humans") make encoding relative to humans. Schulz classifies fooling an LLM monitor with human-readable text as monitor jailbreaking, not encoding. [Skaf et al. 2025](./#p=skaf2025large) ("hidden to an external monitor"; steganographic "with respect to a monitor"), [Emmons et al. 2025 (necessity)](./#p=emmons2025chain) ("a monitor cannot understand"), [Farquhar et al. 2025](./#p=farquhar2025mona) and [Anwar et al. 2026](./#p=anwar2026analyzing) ("the model knows to ignore the mistakes, but the monitor does not") make it relative to the monitor. Load-bearing reasoning that a human can read but a given LLM monitor cannot decode is encoded on the second reading and not on the first.

**Does omission count as obfuscation?** Some definitions count it. [Anwar et al. 2026](./#p=anwar2026analyzing) calls omitting critical steps "obfuscation via suppression". [Imran et al. 2026](./#p=imran2026consistency) counts "the model suppressing mention of extraneous features while remaining influenced by them". [Guan et al. 2025](./#p=guan2025monitoring), [Drori et al. 2025](./#p=drori2025output) and [Schulz 2026](./#p=schulz2026monitor) (obfuscated reasoning as misbehaviour left out of the CoT) also count it. Others do not. [Wang et al. 2026](./#p=wang2026monitorbench) reserves obfuscation for paraphrase, language switching and encoding, and reports that the degradation it observed was omission instead. [Haskins et al. 2026](./#p=haskins2026training) counts as obfuscation only an undetected rate that exceeds a no-reasoning baseline; anything below is reasoning suppression. [Baker et al. 2025](./#p=baker2025monitoring) is on both sides. Its obfuscated reward hacking includes hiding intent by omission. Its obfuscating latent, however, is one that "actively encourages the monitor the believe in the incorrect value of $v$", so a latent that merely omits $v$ is unfaithful, not obfuscating, in its App. G grid. Intentionality also divides the cluster: [Mao et al. 2026](./#p=mao2026taming) defines obfuscation as "non-intentional", while [Korbak et al. 2025](./#p=korbak2025chain) speaks of models that "deliberately obfuscate it".

**Longer or shorter traces?** [Reddy et al. 2025](./#p=reddy2025principled) makes conciseness a dimension of monitorability and scores a trace as monitorable only if it is short, $f(z)=\mathbb{1}_{\text{Length}(z)<950}$ on MATH500. [Anonymous 2026 (CoT reliance)](./#p=anon2026chainofthought) lists "burying the verbalization within a long CoT that is difficult for the monitor to follow" as a monitorability error, and [Arike et al. 2026](./#p=arike2026information) finds that monitors do better with less information. On the other side, [Meek et al. 2025](./#p=meek2025measuring) makes verbosity, "An explicit disclosure of the factors relevant to the task at hand", half of monitorability. [Emmons et al. 2025 (pragmatic)](./#p=emmons2025pragmatic)'s coverage requires all intermediate steps, and [Little 2026](./#p=little2026length) treats length penalties as reducing monitorability. [Li 2026](./#p=li2026monitorability) holds that the effect depends on whether compression rewrites content or removes it. A trace that adds the missing causal factors and grows past 950 tokens gains on Meek's verbosity and loses Reddy's monitorability property.

**Legible to whom?** [Emmons et al. 2025 (pragmatic)](./#p=emmons2025pragmatic) fixes the reader as a human with the user's language. Its rubric follows from that choice: "to receive a high score, the CoT must be in the same language as the original prompt". [Kirchner et al. 2024](./#p=kirchner2024proververifier) uses time-limited human judges. [Roytburg et al. 2026](./#p=roytburg2026measuring) instead requires "some form accessible to weaker monitors" and criticises judge-based scores for relying on strong judges. [Baker et al. 2025](./#p=baker2025monitoring) makes legibility relative to the actual monitor; its example is a CoT in French read by a monitor that only understands English. Under Emmons's rubric, a CoT in a language other than the prompt's scores low whatever the monitor can read; under Baker's, its legibility depends on the monitor.
